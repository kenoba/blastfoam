\begin{abstract}
This report describes the development of a simulation capability for aluminised high explosives, from detonation of the charge to afterburning in air, built on the open-source \texttt{blastFoam} solver suite (OpenFOAM-12). The modelling uses the multi-fluid solver \texttt{blastEulerFoam}, with separate phases for the detonation products, the aluminium particles and the surrounding reactive gas. The report sets out the physical problem and the models chosen for each part of it, distinguishes the capability that existed in \texttt{blastFoam} from what was added or changed in this work, describes the verification, and assesses the status against the aims. The detonation and expansion of the products, their transfer to a reactive gas phase with equilibrium chemistry at high temperature, a subgrid model of the products/air mixing, and the heating, ignition and burning of the aluminium particles are in the solver, each verified in 0D or 1D tests. The model is validated against FOI's 2013 trials of 1\,kg TNT and TNT/Al 80/20 charges fired 2\,m above the ground, using 1D spherical simulations mapped onto 2D axisymmetric meshes with the ground. With the mixing constant set by the secondary-shock timing of the TNT shot, the TNT blast at 6\,m agrees within 0.1\,ms in arrival, 0.5\% in peak overpressure and 5\% in impulse. The effect of the aluminium (earlier arrival of 53--107\,$\mu$s and 27\% more impulse at 4--6\,m, most of it 2--3.5\,ms behind the shock) is reproduced within 3\,$\mu$s in arrival at all three gauges and within 3--4 percentage points in impulse at 4--6\,m, but only with an ignition delay of about 0.5\,ms that is fitted to these shots and lies within the range reported for aluminium in detonation products. The other heights of burst of the trials are the next, independent test.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Aim}

The aim of this work is a validated simulation method for the detonation of an aluminised explosive charge and the subsequent blast and afterburning in air. The process has four parts, each with its own physics and time scale:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item detonation of the charge and early expansion of the detonation products, governed by a high-pressure equation of state (microseconds);
    \item mixing of the products with the surrounding air and finite-rate combustion of the fuel-rich products (CO, H$_2$, \dots) in this mixture (tens of microseconds to milliseconds);
    \item transport, heating, ignition and combustion of the aluminium particles dispersed in the expanding gas (hundreds of microseconds to milliseconds);
    \item afterburning of the aluminium in the mixture of products and air, which raises the late-time pressure and the positive impulse.
\end{enumerate}
The final target is a multi-dimensional simulation of an aluminised charge whose peak overpressure, arrival time and positive impulse agree with gauge data (within 8\%, Section~\ref{sec:status}). The models were developed on a Composition~B charge with 30\% aluminium by mass (Comp-B/Al). Since no gauge data were found for Comp-B/Al, the validation uses FOI's 2013 trials of TNT and TNT/Al 80/20 charges \cite{fedina2014,fedina2017} (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}): pairs of shots with and without aluminium at the same height of burst isolate the effect of the aluminium, which is the part of the problem the model is meant to predict.

\subsection{Approach}

The work builds on \texttt{blastFoam} \cite{synthetik}, an OpenFOAM-based suite for high-explosive detonation and airblast. Two of its solvers are relevant. \texttt{blastFoam} solves multiphase flows with a single mixture velocity and energy; \texttt{blastEulerFoam} is a multi-fluid (Euler--Euler) solver in which each phase has its own mass, momentum and energy equations, coupled through finite-rate exchange terms. Since the aluminium particles lag the gas, heat up at their own rate and burn after a delay, they are modelled as a separate phase, and the work therefore uses \texttt{blastEulerFoam}. The development is staged from zero-dimensional (0D) unit tests through one-dimensional (1D) planar and spherical cases to multi-dimensional simulations, with an automated test suite at each level (Section~\ref{sec:verification}).

The 1D and 2D validation runs are made on a Linux cluster with Slurm (192-core nodes; a 2D FOI shot takes about 8\,h on one node). External tools are used for reference data: the ideal detonation code \texttt{EuDetCode} (chemical equilibrium, CJ state, expansion isentropes and JWL fits; a separate code, not part of the \texttt{blastFoam} repository) and the 1D multiphase code \texttt{gunIB1D} for cross-solver comparison of granular shock problems.

\subsection{Structure of the report}

Section~\ref{sec:framework} describes the multi-fluid modelling framework and lists what was pre-existing and what was developed in this work. Sections~\ref{sec:products}--\ref{sec:mixing} cover the physical models for the four parts of the problem: the explosive and its products, the transfer of the products to a reactive gas, the aluminium particles, and mixing and afterburning. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} describes the numerical methods, including the changes needed to make the multi-fluid solver conservative and stable for this problem, the time integration of the transported particle fields and the two-stage 1D/2D simulation of a charge above the ground. Section~\ref{sec:verification} describes the verification approach and Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, ending with the validation against the FOI 2013 trials (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}). Section~\ref{sec:status} assesses the status against the aims and gives the plan for the remaining work. Throughout, \up{} marks capability that existed in \texttt{blastFoam} before this work, and \tw{} marks what was added or changed.

\section{Modelling Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Phases}

A Comp-B/Al charge is modelled with three phases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{compBAl}: the explosive and its detonation products, a `detonating' fluid phase in which a reaction progress variable $\lambda$ moves the state from the unreacted explosive (Murnaghan EOS) to the products (\texttt{eJWLC} EOS, Section~\ref{sec:products});
    \item \texttt{Al}: the aluminium particles, a granular phase with a compressible solid EOS (Murnaghan) and kinetic theory for the particle stresses;
    \item \texttt{gas}: the surrounding air and, once transferred, the detonation products as a multicomponent reactive gas (NASA-9 thermodynamics in the reacting cases, JANAF in the inert ones; Abel--Nobel EOS; Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).
\end{itemize}
Initially the charge region holds $\alpha_\text{compBAl} = 0.818$ and $\alpha_\text{Al} = 0.182$ (27\% of the charge mass as inert Al at $1820\,\text{kg/m}^3$; Section~\ref{sec:composition}), and the gas fills the rest of the domain.

\subsection{Governing equations}
\label{sec:equations}

\texttt{blastEulerFoam} \up{} solves, for each phase $k$ with volume fraction $\alpha_k$, density $\rho_k$, velocity $\mathbf{u}_k$, pressure $p_k$ and specific total energy $E_k = e_k + \tfrac12|\mathbf{u}_k|^2$, a set of balance equations of Baer--Nunziato type \cite{baer1986,saurel1999}:
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial \alpha_k\rho_k}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\alpha_k\rho_k\mathbf{u}_k) &= \Gamma_k, \\
    \frac{\partial \alpha_k\rho_k\mathbf{u}_k}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\alpha_k\rho_k\mathbf{u}_k\mathbf{u}_k + \alpha_k p_k \mathbf{I}) &= p_I\nabla\alpha_k + \mathbf{M}_k + \Gamma_k\mathbf{u}_I, \\
    \frac{\partial \alpha_k\rho_k E_k}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\big(\alpha_k(\rho_k E_k + p_k)\mathbf{u}_k\big) &= -p_I\frac{\partial\alpha_k}{\partial t} + \mathbf{M}_k\cdot\mathbf{u}_I + Q_k + \Gamma_k E_I,
\end{align}
where $\Gamma_k$ is the interphase mass transfer, $\mathbf{M}_k$ the interphase momentum transfer (drag), $Q_k$ the interphase heat transfer, and $p_I$, $\mathbf{u}_I$, $E_I$ are interfacial values set per phase pair (\texttt{interfacialPressure}, \texttt{interfacialVelocity}). The volume fractions sum to one; the fluid volume fractions are advected with the mixture velocity, and the granular phase adds the kinetic-theory stresses (granular temperature $\Theta$, Lun et al.\ collisional pressure, Johnson--Jackson frictional stress \cite{gidaspow}). Species mass fractions in the gas are transported with the gas mass flux and react through finite-rate chemistry (Section~\ref{sec:gasphase}).

\subsection{Interphase coupling}
\label{sec:coupling}

The phases exchange momentum and energy through finite-rate models and through relaxation steps applied after each stage of the time integration:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Particle drag and heat transfer} \up: Gidaspow--Ergun--Wen--Yu drag and Ranz--Marshall heat transfer between the Al and each fluid phase, i.e.\ with the products and with the gas. Which model of a pair applies is selected by the blending method; with three phases the continuous phase is chosen by precedence (gas before products, \texttt{continuousPhases (gas compBAl)}) \tw, so that the Al is dispersed in both fluids and the products are dispersed in the gas. (With a single continuous phase, the gas, the Al--products models had no weight and the Al received neither drag nor heat from the products.) The Loth drag and the Kavanau heat transfer \tw{} depend on the particle Mach and Knudsen numbers (Section~\ref{sec:al_impl}).
    \item \emph{Products/gas velocity}: the two fluid phases are relaxed instantaneously to a common velocity (\texttt{VRelaxation instant}) \up; the energy update was made conservative \tw{} (Section~\ref{sec:multiphase_numerics}).
    \item \emph{Products/gas pressure and temperature} \tw: in cells where the products and the gas are numerically mixed, the volumes of the non-carrier fluid phases are exchanged until the pressures agree (\texttt{equilibrateDiluteVolumes}, with the $\bar p\,\Delta\alpha$ work moved between the phase energies), and the temperatures are relaxed by a stiff constant-coefficient heat transfer integrated exactly. The upstream pressure-relaxation solver collapses the time step in these cells and is not used.
    \item \emph{Mass transfer}: products $\to$ gas by the \texttt{pressurePhaseChange} model \tw{} (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}); Al $\to$ gas by the reacting-particle model with an empirical burn rate \up, limited to gas-phase oxidiser \tw{} (Section~\ref{sec:al_impl}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Explosive Charge and Detonation Products}
\label{sec:products}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Composition and treatment of the aluminium}
\label{sec:composition}

In an aluminised explosive the charge is a mixture of the high explosive and aluminium particles. Micron-sized particles ($d \gtrsim 1\,\mu\text{m}$) react little in the detonation zone ($\sim 10$--$100\,\text{ns}$); they act as a momentum and heat sink that lowers the detonation velocity and pressure, and burn later in the expanding products and in air \cite{beckstead}. Nano-sized particles can react partly in the early expansion.

The inert part of the aluminium is modelled as a separate granular phase. This lets the particles develop their own velocity and temperature, which matters for the ignition delay and the spatial distribution of the later energy release. It also means that the products EOS must describe the products alone: the thermochemical calculation includes the aluminium in the reactants, but the JWL-type fit is made to the products isentrope with the volume of the inert aluminium excluded, so that the aluminium is not counted twice. For the Comp-B/Al charge (70\% Comp-B, 30\% Al), 90\% of the aluminium is taken as inert in the detonation, i.e.\ 27\% of the charge mass, giving $\alpha_\text{Al} = 0.182$ and a products-phase initial density $\rho_0 = 1624.45\,\text{kg/m}^3$.

\subsection{Programmed burn}

The detonation is not resolved; the reaction front is prescribed (programmed burn). In the \texttt{nodeProgrammed} activation model \tw, the arrival times $t = t_0 + |\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_\text{ign}|/D_\text{CJ}$ at the vertices of each cell give a start and end time $t_\text{start}$, $t_\text{end}$, and the progress variable ramps linearly across the cell,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda = \min\left(1, \max\left(0, \frac{t - t_\text{start}}{t_\text{end} - t_\text{start}}\right)\right),
\end{equation}
optionally also triggered by compression ahead of the front (\texttt{PROGRAMMEDBETA}). The detonating phase blends the unreacted and products states with $\lambda$. Because the front speed is imposed, a correct detonation velocity in a simulation is not a test of the model; what can be tested is the pressure and flow behind the front, which depend on the EOS and on the resolution (Section~\ref{sec:res_spherical}). The detonation velocity $D_\text{CJ}$ and pressure $p_\text{CJ}$ come from \texttt{EuDetCode}.

\subsection{The eJWLC equation of state}
\label{sec:ejwlc}

The products are described by the energy-based Mie--Gr\"uneisen form \texttt{eJWLC} \tw, built on the three-term (JWLC) isentrope through the CJ state. With $V = \rho_0/\rho$,
\begin{align}
    p_s(V) &= A\,e^{-R_1 V} + B\,e^{-R_2 V} + C\,V^{-(\omega+1)}, \\
    e_s(V) &= v_0\left[\frac{A}{R_1}e^{-R_1 V} + \frac{B}{R_2}e^{-R_2 V} + \frac{C}{\omega}V^{-\omega}\right] + E_0, \\
    p(\rho, e) &= p_s(V) + \omega\rho\,\big(e - e_s(V)\big),
\end{align}
where $e_s$ follows from $\mathrm{d}e_s = -p_s\,\mathrm{d}v$ along the isentrope, $v_0 = 1/\rho_0$, and $\omega$ is the (constant) Gr\"uneisen coefficient. The constant $E_0$ sets the energy datum; it does not change $p(\rho, e)$ for given $e - e_s$, and is used to put the products on the same datum as the gas phase (Section~\ref{sec:reconciliation}).

JWL-type equations are mechanical: they relate $p$, $v$ and $e$ but do not define a temperature. A temperature is needed for the heat transfer to the particles and to the gas. \texttt{eJWLC} uses a power law along the isentrope and a constant heat capacity off it:
\begin{equation}
    T_s(V) = T_\text{CJ}\left(\frac{V_\text{CJ}}{V}\right)^{\omega_T}, \qquad T(\rho, e) = T_s(V) + \frac{e - e_s(V)}{C_v},
\end{equation}
with $T_\text{CJ}$ and $V_\text{CJ} = \rho_0/\rho_\text{CJ}$ from \texttt{EuDetCode}. The exponent $\omega_T$ defaults to $\omega$; since it does not enter the pressure, it can be chosen so that $T_s$ also passes through a second known state, here the temperature of the gas formed at the transfer pressure (Section~\ref{sec:reconciliation}). With $\omega_T \neq \omega$ the closure is not thermodynamically consistent with a constant-$C_v$ Mie--Gr\"uneisen EOS; it is a temperature closure only.

\subsection{Parameters for Comp-B/Al}
\label{sec:fit}

The parameters are fitted to the CJ state and expansion isentrope from \texttt{EuDetCode} (BKW EOS, \texttt{Gas24CHNO} database, reactants RDX 0.42, TNT 0.28, Al 0.30 by mass, 90\% of the Al inert). Table~\ref{tab:jwl_parameters} lists the fit to the products alone, used in all Comp-B/Al cases, and the fit to the full mixture for comparison; Figure~\ref{fig:isentrope_comparison} shows the isentropes. The TNT products are strongly fuel-rich: 17.5\% of the transferred mass is graphite and 27\% CO, against 0.3\% H$_2$, so the products afterburning in air (CO and graphite to CO$_2$) is larger than for Comp-B, and the subgrid mixing that controls it (Section~\ref{sec:mixing_model}) has a correspondingly larger effect on the blast. The aluminium particles see the gas-phase oxidisers only after the products have been transferred to the gas; the H$_2$O and CO$_2$ of the products are available to them from then on, the O$_2$ only where air has mixed in.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:jwl_limits}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Range.} The fit covers expansion to $V/V_\text{CJ} \approx 10$--$20$. Beyond that the constant-$\omega$ tail is not reliable, which is one reason for transferring the products to a gas EOS (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).
    \item \emph{Temperature.} The power law with constant exponent does not follow the equilibrium isentrope temperature: along the expansion the equilibrium temperature falls faster, and the power law with $\omega_T = \omega$ is 550--820\,K too high between 0.1 and 1\,GPa (Figure~\ref{fig:temperature_comparison_products}). With the calibrated $\omega_T$ the error is reduced to 270--630\,K there and changes sign near the transfer pressure ($-80$\,K at 100\,bar, $-190$\,K at 20\,bar). The constant $C_v$ ignores shifting equilibria and vibrational excitation. Temperature errors of this size affect the heat transfer to the particles and therefore the ignition delay.
\end{itemize}

\section{Transfer of the Products to a Reactive Gas Phase}
\label{sec:transfer}
% ============================================================

The detonating phase does not carry species. To model combustion of the fuel-rich products with air, the products are transferred, once expanded, to the multicomponent gas phase with a frozen product composition.

\subsection{Transfer criterion}

The transfer is triggered by pressure rather than temperature. The eJWLC pressure follows the equilibrium isentrope to within about 5\% between 2\,GPa and 20\,bar for this fit, while its temperature is in error by hundreds of kelvin (Section~\ref{sec:jwl_limits}), so a temperature threshold would trigger at the wrong state. A pressure window also corresponds to the physical freeze-out of the product chemistry, which occurs at roughly 50--200\,bar as the expansion becomes faster than the recombination reactions.

\subsection{The \texttt{pressurePhaseChange} model}
\label{sec:ppc}

The \texttt{pressurePhaseChange} model \tw{} transfers mass from a source phase (the products) to one or more target phases at the rate
\begin{equation}
    \dot m = \frac{\alpha_s\rho_s}{\tau}\,\Gamma(p)\,\lambda_s,
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is a relaxation time, $\lambda_s$ the burn fraction of the source, and $\Gamma(p)$ a smooth window between $p_\text{lower}$ and $p_\text{upper}$ of width $p_\text{range}$,
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma(p) = \tfrac14\left(1 + \tanh\frac{p_\text{upper} - p}{p_\text{range}}\right)\left(1 + \tanh\frac{p - p_\text{lower}}{p_\text{range}}\right),
\end{equation}
cut off two widths outside the window (a one-sided form is used for $p_\text{lower} = 0$). The transferred mass is distributed over the target species with prescribed mass or mole fractions (the frozen \texttt{EuDetCode} composition at freeze-out). In \texttt{blastEulerFoam} the transferred mass carries the source velocity and specific total energy, and the volume moves with the mass; mass, momentum and total energy are then conserved by the transfer itself. The energy datums of the two phases must agree; this is arranged offline through $E_0$ (Section~\ref{sec:reconciliation}), and no state-dependent energy correction is applied at run time.

\subsection{The gas phase}
\label{sec:gasphase}

The gas phase is a multicomponent mixture of air and product species. In the reacting Comp-B/Al cases it has 24 species (O$_2$, N$_2$, CO, CO$_2$, H$_2$O, H$_2$, NH$_3$, CH$_4$, C$_2$H$_6$, C(gr), Al, Al$_2$O$_3$(s) and the dissociation and intermediate species O, N, NO, H, OH, AlO, HO$_2$, H$_2$O$_2$, CH$_3$, CH$_2$O, HCO, CH$_3$O) with NASA-9 thermodynamics to 20\,000\,K; the inert cases use the first twelve with JANAF 7-coefficient thermodynamics. Transport is Sutherland and the EOS Abel--Nobel (covolume)
\begin{equation}
    p = \frac{\rho R_\text{mix} T}{1 - b\rho}, \qquad b = \sum_k Y_k b_k,
\end{equation}
which accounts for the excluded volume of the dense product gas and tends to the ideal gas for $b\rho \ll 1$. Species are transported with the gas mass flux. Above $T_\text{eq} = 2500$\,K the gas is set to local chemical equilibrium at its density and internal energy (element-potential solver, condensed species as pure phases); below, it reacts by finite-rate chemistry (a reversible GRI-3.0 H$_2$/O$_2$/CO/C1 subset, 68 reactions, and global Al oxidation steps; laminar, integrated with a stiff ODE solver). The multicomponent and reacting thermo for an individual phase of a multiphase system was added in this work; the underlying JANAF, Abel--Nobel and chemistry models are upstream.

\emph{High temperatures.} The JANAF fits of the air species end at 5000\,K. In \texttt{blastFoam} the properties were evaluated at the clamped temperature, so the gas could not exceed 5000\,K and the energy of strongly shocked air was misrepresented. In this work the properties are extrapolated with constant $c_p$ above the fit range, with enthalpy and entropy extended consistently, and the decode temperature cap is raised to 50\,000\,K. This removes the cap but not the physics that is missing: there is no dissociation, so strongly shocked air is too hot (up to 14\,400\,K in the spherical cases and 24\,800\,K in a planar case, where equilibrium air would stay near 10\,000\,K or below). NASA-9 thermodynamics to 20\,000\,K (\texttt{nasa9} thermo, species N$_2$, O$_2$, NO, N, O, H, OH and the product species) and local chemical equilibrium above $T_\text{eq} = 2500$\,K in the gas chemistry (element-potential solver at fixed $(e, \rho)$, verified against \texttt{EuDetCode}) are now available; the shock-in-air test uses them (Section~\ref{sec:res_1d}), and the reacting Comp-B/Al cases use them too (Section~\ref{sec:res_spherical}).

\subsection{Reconciliation of the two equations of state}
\label{sec:reconciliation}

The two phases measure energy from different datums. The JANAF datum includes the heats of formation, so the frozen product gas has a large negative energy; the eJWLC datum is arbitrary (the constant $E_0$). If the datums are not reconciled, mass transferred at constant specific energy arrives in the gas at the wrong temperature and pressure; with $E_0 = 0$ the pressure after transfer at 100\,bar would be about 50\% too high.

The matching is done at the transfer pressure $p_\text{tr} = 100\,\text{bar}$, on the products isentrope ($v_\text{tr} = 2.838\times10^{-2}\,\text{m}^3/\text{kg}$):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Pressure continuity.} The gas temperature at $(p_\text{tr}, v_\text{tr})$ follows from the Abel--Nobel EOS of the frozen composition ($R_\text{mix} = 324.35\,\text{J/(kg\,K)}$, $b = 1.263\times10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3/\text{kg}$): $T_\text{tr} = p_\text{tr}(v_\text{tr} - b)/R_\text{mix} = 836.10\,\text{K}$.
    \item \emph{Energy datum.} $E_0$ is chosen so that the eJWLC energy at $v_\text{tr}$ equals the gas energy at $T_\text{tr}$ on the \texttt{blastFoam} gas convention, $e_\text{gas} = \sum_k Y_k(h_{s,k} - R_k T) = 0.4341\,\text{MJ/kg}$; this gives $E_0 = -503.47\,\text{kJ/kg}$.
    \item \emph{Temperature continuity.} $\omega_T$ is chosen so that $T_s(V_\text{tr}) = T_\text{tr}$ with $T_\text{CJ}$ unchanged:
    \begin{equation*}
        \omega_T = \frac{\ln(T_\text{CJ}/T_\text{tr})}{\ln(V_\text{tr}/V_\text{CJ})} = 0.3711.
    \end{equation*}
\end{enumerate}
The procedure is implemented in \path{utilities/thermo_fitter/products_only_fit.py} \tw. It gives exact pressure and temperature continuity at the matching state; elsewhere in the transfer window the mismatch depends on how closely the two EOS agree. A further step, matching the slope of the isentrope ($C^1$ continuity) by setting the asymptotic $\omega$ to $\gamma_\text{mix} - 1$ of the frozen gas, is implemented in the fitter (\path{fit_ejwlc_nobleabel.py}) but is not used in the current fit, which keeps the \texttt{EuDetCode} value of $\omega$. Figure~\ref{fig:pv_curve_comparison} compares the two descriptions along the expansion.

A state-dependent energy correction (e.g.\ the difference between the two EOS energies at the local temperature) is deliberately not used: it adds energy in proportion to the JWL temperature error. In a 0D test it added about 480\,kJ/kg and raised the pressure by 56\%.

\subsection{Status}

The transfer is verified in a 0D reactor and in a planar detonation (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Since 2026-10-14 it is enabled in the reacting and Al-combustion spherical cases (below 100\,bar, $\tau = 20\,\mu$s, with a stiff \texttt{seulex} chemistry integrator; the explicit RKCK45 integrator failed on the Al reactions); the products then burn with the air (Section~\ref{sec:res_spherical}). \section{Aluminium Particles}
\label{sec:aluminium}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Physical processes}

\paragraph{Heating, melting and ignition.} The particles are heated by convection from the products and the shocked air. Aluminium melts at 933\,K ($\Delta H_\text{fus} \approx 397\,\text{kJ/kg}$), which holds the particle temperature during melting. The particles are coated with an oxide shell that prevents combustion until it fails, at about 2050--2300\,K near the oxide melting point; the ignition temperature depends on the heating rate and the environment. At the heating rates behind a blast wave ($h \sim 10^4$--$10^5\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{K)}$) the particle interior lags its surface, which ignites first.

\paragraph{Combustion.} Micron-sized particles burn with a detached vapour-phase diffusion flame; the burn time scales as $t_b \propto d^n$ with $n \approx 1.8$--$2$. Nano-sized particles burn by surface kinetics with $n \approx 1$. A widely used correlation for micron particles in mixed oxidisers is that of Beckstead \cite{beckstead},
\begin{equation}
    t_b\,[\text{ms}] = \frac{a\,d^{\,n}}{X_\text{eff}\,p^{0.1}\,T_0^{0.2}}, \qquad X_\text{eff} = X_{\text{O}_2} + 0.6X_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} + 0.22X_{\text{CO}_2},
    \label{eq:beckstead}
\end{equation}
with $a = 0.00735$, $n = 1.8$, $d$ in $\mu$m, $p$ in atm and the ambient temperature $T_0$ in K.
For the 20--40\,$\mu$m powders of typical aluminised explosives the correlation gives 1.6--5.5\,ms in air at 2800\,K, so the aluminium is essentially unburnt during the detonation and early expansion and releases its energy in the fireball.

\paragraph{Reactions and energy.} The aluminium reacts with O$_2$ ($31\,\text{MJ/kg}$ Al to Al$_2$O$_3$), H$_2$O ($15.4\,\text{MJ/kg}$) and CO$_2$ ($13.6\,\text{MJ/kg}$). The reactions with H$_2$O and CO$_2$ allow burning inside the products before air is entrained. The flame forms gaseous sub-oxides and Al$_2$O$_3$ vapour, which condense to sub-micron oxide smoke; the condensation (about 18--19\,MJ/kg of oxide) is a large part of the heat release and holds the flame temperature near the oxide boiling point (about 3800--4000\,K at elevated pressure). The smoke is a strong emitter, so radiation can be a significant loss from the fireball (Section~\ref{sec:radiation}).

\subsection{Ignition in detonation products}
\label{sec:al_ignition}

The ignition criteria above come from particles heated slowly in quiescent or low-speed gas. Behind a detonation the particles are heated in a high-speed flow of products that cool rapidly by expansion, and the evidence for a well-defined ignition temperature there is weak. This subsection summarises the literature and its consequence for the model.

\paragraph{Shock-tube and torch data.} Gurevich et al.\ \cite{gurevich1970} measured ignition temperatures from about 2330\,K (near the oxide melting point) for large particles down to 969\,K for 6\,$\mu$m particles near stoichiometric conditions. In reflected shock waves Boiko and Poplavski \cite{boiko2002} found two regimes: self-ignition above 1800\,K, and a ``low-temperature'' ignition at 1000--1800\,K in which only a small, irreproducible part of the particles ignites, with delays scattered between 0.4 and 2\,ms, which can nevertheless ignite the whole cloud. Kuhl and Boiko \cite{kuhlboiko2010} fitted the self-ignition delays of 3--5\,$\mu$m spheres (1.1--2.5\,MPa, 1670--2170\,K) as
\begin{equation}
    \tau_\text{ign}\,[\text{ms}] = 2.5\times10^{-8}\,\exp(30\,000\,\text{K}/T),
    \label{eq:kuhlboiko}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ 1.1\,ms at 1700\,K, 0.43\,ms at 1800\,K and 0.08\,ms at 2000\,K (flakes 60\% shorter), and a thermal-explosion model gives a size dependence $\tau_\text{ign}\,[\text{ms}] = 8.6\times10^{-4}\,d^{2.3}$ ($d$ in $\mu$m; 53\,$\mu$s at 6\,$\mu$m, 2.1\,ms at 30\,$\mu$m). They integrate the delay along the particle paths as an ignition progress $\mathrm{d}f/\mathrm{d}t = 1/\tau_\text{ign}(T)$ and add a cloud-concentration ignition probability (50\% at about 130\,g/m$^3$); Balakrishnan et al.\ \cite{balakrishnan2012} applied this model to explosively dispersed clouds and found that shorter delays burn more of the cloud. For 40\,$\mu$m particles behind reflected shocks in air, Omang and Hauge \cite{omang2022} observed cloud ignition above 635\,K, with $\tau_\text{ign}\,[\text{ms}] = 0.31\exp(15.67\,\text{kJ/mol}/(R T_5))$ (about 2\,ms at 1000\,K and 1.1\,ms at 1500\,K). All these delays include the heating of the particles.

\paragraph{Particles in detonation products.} Tanguay et al.\ \cite{tanguay2009,tanguay2009thesis} exposed 2--100\,$\mu$m aluminium powders (Valimet H-series) to the flow of stoichiometric H$_2$/O$_2$ detonation products at atmospheric pressure. The particles emitted light almost from the passage of the shock, without a sharp ignition or extinction event; the reaction delay (half of the peak luminosity) was 0.07--0.4\,ms for H10, 0.4--0.7\,ms for H30, 0.5--0.7\,ms for H50 and 0.67--0.97\,ms for H95 particles, and lay for the larger sizes between the computed times for the particles to reach the melting points of aluminium (about 0.03\,ms at 30\,$\mu$m) and of the oxide. The reaction duration grew only as about $d^{0.5}$ and the combustion temperature (2200--2800\,K) was below the flow temperature, which indicates reaction in the kinetic rather than the diffusive regime. The authors conclude that a critical ignition temperature is ill-posed for aluminium in detonation products, and that the particles react throughout their exposure. In the analytical model of Tanguay et al.\ \cite{tanguay2007} most of the particle heating occurs in the Taylor wave and the quiescent region behind it, after which the expanding products become cooler than the particles; this gives a critical charge diameter for particle ignition that grows as $d^{1.5}$--$d^2$.

\paragraph{Condensed explosives.} Frost et al.\ \cite{frost2002,frost2006} detonated cylindrical packed beds of aluminium particles (3--114\,$\mu$m, 73--77\% by mass) saturated with sensitised nitromethane in 9--74\,mm tubes. With increasing charge diameter the dispersed particles show no reaction, reaction at isolated spots, bands and rings that start at perturbations of the particle cone where the particles mix with the shock-heated air, and continuous reaction of the cloud. The most reactive size was about 54\,$\mu$m (continuous reaction at 48--74\,mm), whereas 114\,$\mu$m particles did not react in any charge; where the particles reacted, AlO emission appeared about 50\,$\mu$s behind the detonation front. Below the critical diameter, the reaction is delayed beyond about 1\,ms, too late to reinforce the blast wave \cite{tanguay2009thesis}. The behaviour of spherical charges is more gradual, and Tanguay \cite{tanguay2009thesis} concludes that there is no ignition temperature for aluminium in high-explosive detonation products. In the computations of Posey et al.\ \cite{posey2021}, which assume that the oxide shell is fractured during dispersal (no ignition temperature), the ignition of an aluminium shell dispersed by a TNT charge is delayed for large particles or high particle mass: the particles are first pushed out of the hot products, and those carried back into the fireball during the negative phase ignite a dust flame that spreads through the cloud.

\paragraph{Consequence for the model.} The literature thus supports (i) reaction starting near the melting point rather than at the oxide melting point, (ii) a delay of the order of 0.1--1\,ms for 30--50\,$\mu$m particles, which grows with particle size and falls with gas temperature, and (iii) burning mainly at the dilute edge of the particle cloud, where it mixes with hot air. The reacting diameter model therefore has an optional ignition delay $\tau_\text{ign}$: an ignition progress, transported with the particles like the ignited fraction, grows at $1/\tau_\text{ign}$ while the particle temperature is at or above $T_\text{ign}$, and the particles ignite when it reaches one (the Kuhl--Boiko form with a constant delay). For the TNT/Al charge of the FOI 2013 trials (shot 5, TNT/Al 80/20 with 42\,$\mu$m particles; Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}) the measured effect of the aluminium is a small change of the shock arrival and an impulse gain 2--3.5\,ms behind the shock, i.e.\ of the delayed kind. With $T_\text{ign} = 933$\,K, a burning cut-off of $\alpha_\text{Al} = 10^{-9}$ and $\tau_\text{ign} = 0.5$\,ms the 2D axisymmetric model reproduces the measured shot 5 -- shot 4 differences in arrival time (within 3\,$\mu$s at three gauges) and impulse ($+23$--$24$\% against $+27$\%); $\tau_\text{ign} = 0.25$ and 1\,ms move the arrival at the two outer gauges by about $-40$ and $+75$--$95$\,$\mu$s. The value of 0.5\,ms after reaching the melting point lies within the delays measured by Tanguay et al.\ for 36--54\,$\mu$m particles (H30, H50), but it is fitted: those data are for H$_2$/O$_2$ products at atmospheric pressure, and the prompt reaction (about 50\,$\mu$s) in the nitromethane cylinders of Frost et al.\ shows that the delay depends on the charge size, the aluminium loading and the expansion. A delay that depends on the gas temperature and the particle size, Eq.~(\ref{eq:kuhlboiko}) with a $d^2$ scaling, would carry over to other charges without refitting; the model therefore also accepts $\tau = \tau_\text{ign}\exp(T_{a,\text{ign}}/T')(d/d_\text{ref})^{n}$ with $T'$ the particle or the gas temperature (the Kuhl--Boiko form with $T' = T_\text{gas}$, $T_\text{ign} = 0$), verified in single-cell runs (ignition time within 0.4\%). In the 1D shot 5 run the products around the particles have cooled to 600--850\,K by 0.1\,ms while the particles are at 930--1750\,K, and only the edge of the cloud is in shocked air at about 2400\,K, so a delay in the gas temperature ignites the aluminium mainly at the cloud edge. Tested on shot 5 in 1D (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}, Table~\ref{tab:foi_kb}), this form does not give the delayed ignition that the measurement needs: with the $d^2$ or $d^{2.3}$ scaling from the 3--5\,$\mu$m data the 42\,$\mu$m particles hardly ignite (effect of inert mass), and without it they ignite while the products are hot, as with a constant delay of 0.25\,ms. In 2D (with the $d^2$ scaling) none of the aluminium ignites either. The fitted constant delay is therefore kept.

\subsection{Modelling approaches}
\label{sec:al_approaches}

Four approaches are candidates for the particle phase; their selection is part of the planned work (Section~\ref{sec:status}).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Eulerian multi-fluid} (current): the particles are a continuum phase with its own velocity and temperature. Appropriate in the dense charge ($\alpha_\text{Al} \approx 0.2$) and early expansion; a single size class per phase; numerical diffusion at the edge of the dispersing cloud.
    \item \emph{Lagrangian parcels} (\texttt{blastParcelFoam} \up): particle trajectories with their own size, temperature and burn history; natural polydispersity and no numerical diffusion. Standard implementations neglect the particle volume, which is not acceptable in the dense charge; a dense formulation (gas volume fraction in the carrier equations, particle stress, kernel projection of parcel volume) would be needed.
    \item \emph{Population balance}: size classes (sectional) or transported moments (QMOM) of the size distribution in the Eulerian framework, with burning as a moment source.
    \item \emph{Hybrid}: Eulerian in the dense core, converted conservatively to Lagrangian parcels where $\alpha_\text{Al}$ falls below a threshold (about $10^{-3}$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Current implementation}
\label{sec:al_impl}

In the solver the aluminium is a granular Eulerian phase \up{} with a compressible solid EOS (Murnaghan); the granular energy equation of this work includes the compression energy of the solid EOS (before, the particles cooled to the 100\,K floor behind the detonation). The burn rate is computed by the reacting diameter model \up, which this work has extended as follows; each part has a single-cell test (Section~\ref{sec:res_al}), and the FOI 2013 cases (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}) use all of them:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Melting} (\texttt{eMelting} thermodynamics): constant heat capacities of the solid (1059\,J/(kg\,K), the mean between 298 and 933\,K) and the liquid (1177\,J/(kg\,K)) and the latent heat (396.6\,kJ/kg) released over 2\,K around 933.47\,K (NASA/CODATA data). The energy is piecewise linear in temperature and is inverted exactly; Newton's method on the kinked curve can cycle between the branches.
    \item \emph{Compressible and rarefied drag} (\texttt{Loth}, aluminium in the gas): the correlation of Loth \cite{loth}, a function of the Reynolds number, the particle Mach number $|U_r|/c$ and the Knudsen number $\sqrt{\pi\gamma/2}\,Ma/Re$, with a rarefaction branch (Re $<$ 45) and a compression branch; at $Ma \to 0$ it reduces to Schiller--Naumann with the Clift--Gauvin term. In the dense charge (aluminium in the products) the Gidaspow--Ergun--Wen--Yu drag is kept.
    \item \emph{Rarefied heat transfer} (Ranz--Marshall with the \texttt{Kavanau} Nusselt number, aluminium in the gas): $Nu = Nu_c/(1 + 3.42\,Ma\,Nu_c/(Re\,Pr))$ with $Nu_c = 2 + 0.459\,Re^{0.55}Pr^{0.33}$ \cite{kavanau}. A 1\,$\mu$m particle in air at 0.2\,bar and 2500\,K has $Nu = 0.28$ instead of 2. The Prandtl number of a phase pair was computed with $c_v$ instead of $c_p$ (fixed).
    \item \emph{Beckstead burning} (\texttt{Beckstead} diameter rate): the $d^n$ law of Eq.~(\ref{eq:beckstead}) with $X_\text{eff}$ from the gas composition. It replaces the empirical pressure law of the upstream model ($r_b = a\,(p/1\,\text{MPa})^n$ above 2\,bar), which had no ignition and no oxidiser dependence. Testing it found two errors in the reacting diameter model: the particle number density used the initial diameter, so a burnt-out cloud kept $1/e$ of its mass, and a volume floor of $10^{-15}\,\text{m}^3$ limited the burning of particles below 12\,$\mu$m (fixed).
    \item \emph{Oxidiser limit}: the rate is multiplied by the gas fraction of the surrounding fluid, $\alpha_\text{gas}/(1 - \alpha_\text{Al})$, so that the particles do not burn inside the detonation products, which are a separate phase until they are transferred to the gas.
    \item \emph{Burning to oxide.} The burnt aluminium reacts with the oxidisers of the cell by one global reaction each, $4\text{Al} + 3\text{O}_2 \to 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $2\text{Al} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{H}_2$ and $2\text{Al} + 3\text{CO}_2 \to \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{CO}$, in the shares of their terms in $X_\text{eff}$; the mass transfer adds the products to the gas and removes the oxidisers, and the heat of reaction follows from the formation enthalpies. The oxide is liquid where the gas is above its melting point (2327\,K) and solid below. The rate is limited so that no more than half of an oxidiser in a cell is consumed in a time step, so the burning cannot exceed the available oxygen. (Before, the burnt aluminium entered the gas as vapour, which conserves energy but cools the gas by its formation enthalpy, 12.2\,MJ/kg, and did not react below $T_\text{eq}$.)
    \item \emph{Ignited fraction.} With a single diameter per cell, a flag such as ``the particles burn once $d < d_0$'' spreads by the mixing of the cell-mean diameter. The ignition is therefore tracked by the fraction of the particles that have ignited, transported with the particles and set to one where their temperature reaches $T_\text{ign}$; the burn rate is multiplied by it, and a particle that has ignited keeps burning when it cools below $T_\text{ign}$.
    \item \emph{Ignition delay} (optional): the particles ignite only when an ignition progress, transported like the ignited fraction, reaches one; it grows at $1/\tau$ where they are at or above $T_\text{ign}$, with a constant $\tau$ or $\tau = \tau_\text{ign}\exp(T_{a,\text{ign}}/T')(d/d_\text{ref})^n$ in the particle or the gas temperature $T'$ (Section~\ref{sec:al_ignition}).
    \item \emph{Burning cut-off.} Below a volume fraction \texttt{alphaMin} the diameter is reset to $d_0$, the ignited fraction to zero and the particles do not burn. The default ($10^{-4}$) is reached at about 0.5\,m from a 1\,kg charge, and even $10^{-6}$ stops the burning of the dilute cloud at 2--3\,m (in the FOI shot 5 run 42\% of the aluminium mass was below it at 3\,ms); the FOI cases use $10^{-9}$. The phase \texttt{residualAlpha} and the drag cut-offs stay at $10^{-6}$: at $10^{-9}$ the 1D runs became unstable (time step $5\times10^{-22}$\,s, gas pressure 25\,GPa in a residual cell).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Time integration of the particle fields.} The diameter, the ignited fraction and the ignition progress are cell means transported with the particle volume flux and must be advanced with the blending of the RK2SSP stages (Section~\ref{sec:timeint}). Until 2026-11-21 they were advanced from the unblended stage value, which transports them 1.5 times faster than the particles. The ignited fraction then ran ahead of the particle cloud and was reset there, and the mass transfer, which included the fall of the cell-mean diameter by this transport while the diameter was not allowed to rise, removed particles numerically: in the 1D FOI shot 5 case 23\% of the particles were gone by 1\,ms and 59\% of the aluminium mass by 16\,ms, of which burning explained 9\%. The mass transfer now uses the volume change of a particle by burning alone, $\dot V = \tfrac{\pi}{2}d^2\dot d_\text{burn}$, and the particle number $\sum\alpha V/(\tfrac{\pi}{6}d^3)$ is conserved until the particles burn out. All aluminium results before that date overstated the burning (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}).

These models are verified in single-cell runs (Section~\ref{sec:res_al}). In the gas, cells above $T_\text{eq}$ are brought to equilibrium, so the oxide formed there is partly dissociated or reduced to the sub-oxides (Al$_2$O, AlO, AlO$_2$, Al$_2$O$_2$) and Al vapour. The burning aluminium is not injected as these intermediates: the flame zone (a few particle diameters, about 0.1\,mm) is far smaller than a cell, and below $T_\text{eq}$ there are no aluminium kinetics, so injected intermediates would not react further. Hydroxides (AlOH, HAlO, AlO$_2$H) change the equilibrium of the products by at most 3\,K and hold at most 1.3\% of the aluminium, and are not included. Not in the solver: a particle size distribution (one size per phase), the internal temperature profile of the particle (the reference model's surface ignition), anaerobic reaction with the detonation products before they are transferred to the gas, and a finite-rate description of the oxide condensation.

\section{Mixing, Turbulence and Afterburning}
\label{sec:mixing}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Mixing of products, particles and air}

The products and the aluminium can only burn with air after mixing with it. At the product--air contact the mixing is driven by the Richtmyer--Meshkov instability (shock passage across the density interface, including the reflected shocks), the Rayleigh--Taylor instability (deceleration of the dense products by the air) and Kelvin--Helmholtz instability of the resulting shear layers. At fireball temperatures the gas-phase reactions and the burning of small particles are fast compared with the mixing (Damk\"ohler number $\gg 1$), so the afterburning rate is controlled by the mixing.

The gas and the particles mix differently. Gas-phase products follow the flow and mix down to molecular scales; their combustion is confined to the turbulent mixing layer. Particles have inertia, characterised by the Stokes number $\text{St} = \tau_p/\tau_f$ with $\tau_p = \rho_p d^2/(18\mu_g)$: large particles ($\text{St} > 1$, $d \gtrsim 15$--$30\,\mu\text{m}$) decouple from the decelerating gas and penetrate ahead of the products into fresh air, small particles follow the gas, and particles with $\text{St} \sim 1$ concentrate between vortices. The aluminium can therefore burn in a distributed volume ahead of the gaseous fireball, which sustains the late-time pressure.

These effects are multi-dimensional; in 1D the particle cloud stays inside the products except at its dilute edge (Section~\ref{sec:res_spherical}).

\subsection{Subgrid mixing model}
\label{sec:mixing_model}

At the resolutions affordable for metre-scale domains (millimetres at best) the products/air mixing layer is not resolved: in 1D it does not exist, and in 2D the instabilities grow from the mesh and are not converged (Section~\ref{sec:res_g2}). The laminar 2D run of FOI shot 4 resolves almost none of the products/air mixing (0.5\% of the products fuel burnt). This work therefore adds a gradient-diffusion model of the unresolved mixing, \texttt{turbulentMixingDiffusion} (an \texttt{fvModel}), which adds
\begin{equation}
    \nabla\cdot\left(\alpha\rho\,D(t)\,m\,\nabla\psi\right)
    \label{eq:mixdiff}
\end{equation}
to the species and energy equations ($\psi = Y_k$, $e$) of the gas phase, in flux form, so that species mass and energy are conserved; diffusing the phase energy with the species makes the Lewis number one.

\paragraph{Mixing layer.} The term acts only in the products/air mixing layer, where the mixture fraction of the products, from an inert tracer (N$_2$),
\begin{equation}
    z = \frac{Y_{\text{N}_2} - Y_{\text{N}_2,\text{air}}}{Y_{\text{N}_2,\text{products}} - Y_{\text{N}_2,\text{air}}},
\end{equation}
lies between $z_\text{min}$ and $1 - z_\text{min}$ ($z_\text{min} = 10^{-3}$); the mask $m$ is one there and zero elsewhere, and the face coefficient is interpolated from the masked cell values, so the layer can widen by its own diffusion. Shocks in air and the products core are not touched. $Y_{\text{N}_2,\text{products}}$ is the N$_2$ fraction of the products$\to$gas transfer composition (0.183 for TNT, 0.179 for TNT/Al).

\paragraph{Diffusivity.} The diffusivity follows the growth of the mixing layer, $D = C\,w\,\mathrm{d}w/\mathrm{d}t$, with the layer width fitted to the 3D TNT simulations of Balakrishnan et al.\ \cite{balakrishnan2010},
\begin{equation}
    \frac{w}{r_0} = \max\left(7.4\,\tau,\; 19.7\,\tau^{1.56}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{t\,[\text{ms}]}{W^{1/3}\,[\text{kg}^{1/3}]},
\end{equation}
with $r_0$ the charge radius and $W$ the charge mass (with the aluminium for TNT/Al), up to the end of their implosion phase, $\tau_1 = 0.9$, after which $D$ decays as $\tau_1/\tau$. $D(t)$ is given to the model as a table. The coefficient $C$ is the calibration constant: $C = 0.005$ reproduces the burnt fraction history of the products fuel of Balakrishnan et al.\ in 1D (17.6\% by 3\,ms; $C = 0.015$ gives 33.6\%), and $C = 0.0125$ the measured timing of the secondary shock of the FOI TNT shot (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}). In 1D the secondary shock arrives at 2.83 and 4.47\,m 3.87 and 4.82\,ms after the main shock without mixing, and 3.08/3.84, 2.73/3.38 and 2.19/2.65\,ms with $C$ = 0.005, 0.01 and 0.03 (measured at P1/P2 2.66 and 3.01\,ms); $C$ = 0.01 and 0.015 also advance the main shock at 4.47\,m by 73 and 140\,$\mu$s and raise its impulse by 5 and 10\% relative to $C = 0.005$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}). In the 2D shot 4 case with $C = 0.005$ the mixing raises the P2/P3 impulse by 7\% and the peaks by 4\% over the laminar run.

\paragraph{Species and solver requirements.} With interphase mass transfer into the gas (the products transfer and the aluminium burning), the multicomponent thermo solves every species and normalises the mass fractions. All species of the phase, the default (inert) species included, must therefore be diffused: an omitted N$_2$ tracer was converted into the other species by the normalisation (6\% extra products carbon in the shot 4 runs). The model stops with an error if a species is missing. For the same reason the species solves must be tight: the species are solved one by one, so their solver errors do not sum to zero, and the normalisation turns the difference into element changes. With a tolerance of $10^{-9}$ the gas carbon grew by $5\times10^{-3}$ in 3\,ms, with $10^{-15}$ by $10^{-7}$; the model warns if a species solver has a tolerance above $10^{-12}$ or a relative tolerance. The model is verified in a planar test (\path{tests/1D/verify_turbulent_mixing_diffusion.py}): with $D = kt$ the species profile follows the error-function solution within $10^{-3}$, mass and energy are conserved to $10^{-9}$, the pressure stays uniform within 1\,Pa, a step outside the layer ($z = 1$) is not diffused, and a species list without N$_2$ is rejected.

\paragraph{Limits.} The model is a closure for 1D and under-resolved 2D: it adds the mixing of a 3D TNT fireball as a function of time only, with one constant, and does not distinguish the resolved from the unresolved part of the mixing. With a resolved mixing layer, $D$ would have to be scaled with the cell size. The particles are not diffused (their dispersion is resolved by the particle phase).

\subsection{Turbulence and turbulence--chemistry interaction}

Large-eddy simulation (LES, with e.g.\ WALE or dynamic Smagorinsky subgrid models) is the natural choice for instability-driven mixing, which is dominated by large coherent structures; RANS ($k$--$\omega$ SST with compressibility corrections) is cheaper for parametric studies in 2D. The mean reaction rate cannot be evaluated at the mean temperature and composition; subgrid closures such as the partially stirred reactor (PaSR, rate reduced by $\kappa = \tau_c/(\tau_c + \tau_\text{mix})$) or the eddy dissipation concept account for the unresolved mixing.

\paragraph{Turbulence in \texttt{blastEulerFoam}.} Each fluid phase builds its own momentum transport model \up{} (\texttt{momentumTransport.<phase>}; laminar, RAS or LES from the OpenFOAM library) and a thermophysical transport model. In the implicit part of the phase update the momentum equation gets the divergence of the effective stress, the energy equation its work and the heat flux, and the species equations of the multicomponent gas the diffusive flux (with RAS by default unity Lewis number with the eddy diffusivity). The $k$--$\omega$ SST model of OpenFOAM-12 runs on the gas phase of the Comp-B/Al cases with two adjustments of the case only: $k$, $\omega$, $\nu_t$ and $\alpha_t$ fields with wedge patches, and a trace of gas ($\alpha = 10^{-6}$) in the charge, since the $k$ and $\omega$ matrices have a zero diagonal where the phase is absent and the first Gauss--Seidel sweep raises a floating-point exception.

\paragraph{PaSR.} The PaSR model \up{} multiplies the reaction rates and the heat release of the gas by
\begin{equation}
    \kappa = \frac{\tau_c}{\tau_c + \tau_\text{mix}},
\end{equation}
with the chemical time $\tau_c$ of the chemistry model and, by default, the turbulent mixing time $\tau_\text{mix} = C_\text{mix}\sqrt{\nu_\text{eff}/\varepsilon}$ of the RAS model. Since the gas chemistry sets the cells above $T_\text{eq}$ to equilibrium through a rate $\rho(Y_\text{eq} - Y)/\Delta t$ (Section~\ref{sec:gasphase}), $\kappa$ also slows the approach to equilibrium. This work added a mixing time from the cell size \tw{} (\texttt{mixingTime delta} in \texttt{PaSRCoeffs}):
\begin{equation}
    \tau_\text{mix} = C_\text{mix}\frac{\Delta^2}{\nu_\text{eff} + \nu_\text{sgs}}, \qquad \nu_\text{sgs} = (C_s\Delta)^2\sqrt{2\,\mathbf{D}:\mathbf{D}}, \quad \mathbf{D} = \operatorname{dev}\left(\tfrac12(\nabla\mathbf{u} + \nabla\mathbf{u}^T)\right),
\end{equation}
the time to mix across a cell by molecular (and, with a RAS model, modelled) diffusion and by the Smagorinsky viscosity of the resolved strain ($C_s$ = 0.17). $\Delta$ is twice the mean distance from the cell centre to its faces, without the wedge and empty faces, and is evaluated at every step so that it follows the AMR. Only the deviatoric strain enters: pure expansion or compression of the gas does not mix it. This variant needs no turbulence model; with a laminar model $\nu_\text{eff}$ is the molecular viscosity. The 1D study of Section~\ref{sec:res_g3c} assesses both.

\subsection{Thermal radiation}
\label{sec:radiation}

Radiation from the oxide smoke at 3000--4000\,K can be a significant energy loss from the fireball. The radiative transfer equation can be solved in the P1 or discrete-ordinates approximation with a Planck-mean absorption coefficient of the smoke. Radiation models are available in \path{src/radiationModels} \up, but no solver used here couples them to the multi-fluid energy equations; this would be needed if the radiative loss turns out to be significant.

\section{Numerical Methods}
\label{sec:numerics}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Discretisation}
\label{sec:discretisation}

The equations are solved by a finite-volume Godunov method \up. Each phase has its own approximate Riemann solver; the Comp-B/Al cases use HLLC \cite{toro} for all phases, blended towards HLL near shocks (below), with second-order MUSCL reconstruction (Minmod limiter) and a two-stage strong-stability-preserving Runge--Kutta scheme (RK2SSP). AUSM+, HLL and Kurganov--Tadmor are also available. The time step is limited by the phase Courant numbers (0.2 in the Comp-B/Al cases) and, in this work, by the rate of interphase mass loss. Adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) and load balancing are available \up{} and are used in the 2D axisymmetric Comp-B/Al cases (Sections~\ref{sec:res_g1} and~\ref{sec:res_g2}).

This work rewrote the HLLC phase flux to the standard star-state form $F + S(U^* - U)$ with separate owner and neighbour star states, an explicit branch for left-moving waves and a guarded contact speed.

The HLLC flux resolves contact and shear waves exactly, and therefore does not damp transverse perturbations of a strong shock aligned with the mesh (the carbuncle). In the 2D axisymmetric case this appears along the axis and the symmetry plane (Section~\ref{sec:res_g1}). Near shocks the mass, momentum and energy fluxes are therefore blended towards the HLL flux, with the weight $1 - f^3$, where $f$ is the smallest pressure ratio $\min(p_i/p_j, p_j/p_i)$ between a cell of the face and its neighbours (the sensor of the HLLC-type solver of Xie et al.\ \cite{xie2018}), evaluated with the mixture pressure. Faces with a trace phase keep the HLLC flux. The blend is the default since 2026-10-20 (\texttt{hybridHLL}); it raises the air-shock peaks of the 1D spherical case by 1--2\%.

\subsection{Time integration}
\label{sec:timeint}

The phase equations are advanced by a two-stage strong-stability-preserving Runge--Kutta scheme (RK2SSP) \up. Each stage evaluates the change $L(U)$ of the conserved fields from the current stage values and sets the new values to a blend of the stored old stage values plus $\Delta t$ times a blend of the stored stage changes,
\begin{equation}
    U^{(1)} = U^n + \Delta t\,L(U^n), \qquad U^{(2)} = U^{n+1} = \tfrac12 U^n + \tfrac12\left(U^{(1)} + \Delta t\,L(U^{(1)})\right),
\end{equation}
i.e.\ old-value weights $(1)$ and $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and change weights $(1)$ and $(0, \tfrac12)$; terms treated implicitly (e.g.\ the species and energy diffusion) are solved in a separate implicit stage. Every field that is advanced with the phases must use the same blending, including fields that are transported but not conserved, such as the cell-mean particle diameter $d$, the ignited fraction and the ignition progress of the aluminium (Section~\ref{sec:al_impl}), which obey $\partial_t\psi + \nabla\cdot(\phi\psi) - \psi\nabla\cdot\phi = S_\psi$ with the particle volume flux $\phi$. Advancing such a field from the unblended current stage value, $\psi^{(2)} = \psi^{(1)} + \tfrac12\Delta t\,L(\psi^{(1)})$, transports it $1.5$ times faster than the phase that carries it. A bounded field transported in this way leaves its carrier and is reset or clipped where the carrier is absent; for the aluminium this removed particles numerically until it was corrected (Section~\ref{sec:al_impl}). The helper functions of the time-integration system (\texttt{storeAndBlendOld}, \texttt{storeAndBlendDelta}) store the fields by name, so each blended field and its change need distinct names.

The species of a multicomponent phase are solved one at a time and the mass fractions are then normalised to sum to one where the phase receives mass transfer. The solver errors of the individual species do not cancel, and the normalisation turns their sum into a change of the element composition. With the subgrid mixing model (Section~\ref{sec:mixing_model}) a species tolerance of $10^{-9}$ changed the gas carbon by $5\times10^{-3}$ in 3\,ms; the cases use $10^{-15}$ without a relative tolerance.

The time step is limited by the phase Courant numbers (0.2) and by a Courant number based on the interphase mass transfer, so that the burning and the products transfer remove only part of a phase's mass in a step.

\subsection{Multiphase numerics: conservation and trace phases}
\label{sec:multiphase_numerics}

A multi-fluid solver must handle cells where a phase is absent or present only as a trace, which in a detonation problem is most of the domain for each phase. The upstream treatment and the first version of this work reset dilute phases ($\alpha < 0.01$) towards reference states and wrote the result back to the conserved variables. This destroyed energy (1--2\% in the 0D transfer test) and removed the energy of the products expanding into the air. The following changes were made \tw; together they make the solver conservative and keep trace phases bounded.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Conservative decode.} The conserved variables $\alpha\rho$, $\alpha\rho\mathbf{u}$, $\alpha\rho E$ are modified only where a phase is residual ($\alpha \le$ \texttt{residualAlpha}, $10^{-6}$); bounds on velocity and temperature act on the primitive variables only. The decoded density is no longer bounded below by a reference density, which had held expanded gas at ambient density and overstated its pressure up to twenty-fold.
    \item \emph{Pressure of trace phases.} Without pressure relaxation, all fluid phases in a cell undergo the same volumetric strain, so a trace of one fluid in another acquires an unrelated density and pressure. Non-carrier fluid phases are brought to pressure equilibrium with the carrier by a damped Newton volume exchange, $\Delta\alpha = (p_k - p_c)/(\rho_k c_k^2/\alpha_k + \rho_c c_c^2/\alpha_c)$, with the work $\bar p\,\Delta\alpha$ moved between the phase energies. Trial states outside the range of an EOS (e.g.\ gas compressed past its covolume) are rejected. In numerically mixed products/air cells the temperatures are relaxed with a stiff heat transfer integrated exactly; without it, products that are a trace by mass reached $10^5\,\text{K}$.
    \item \emph{Energy of the relaxation and drag.} The instantaneous velocity relaxation added the dissipated kinetic energy to the total energy a second time; it now keeps each phase's internal energy plus its own dissipation. The work of the implicit drag is evaluated at the interfacial velocity averaged over the update.
    \item \emph{Trace phases in the Riemann fluxes.} A phase that is a trace on one side of a face was given its own impedance in the HLLC flux. For a trace of explosive in an air cell this impedance is that of the explosive, and a small velocity of the air caused large face-pressure changes that destabilised the charge/air interface. Where a fluid phase is a trace on a side ($\alpha <$ \texttt{alphaTrace} $= 10^{-3}$, blended linearly), the wave speeds and the common star pressure now use the mixture density, sound speed, velocity and pressure of that side, while the fluxes still carry the phase's own mass and energy; a residual upwind side carries no flux. A stationary unreacted charge next to air then stays at rest (Section~\ref{sec:res_spherical}).
    \item \emph{Energy of the particle coupling and of the implicit stage.} A closed-domain energy check found three errors, all in pre-existing code. (i) The drag ODE solver moved momentum between the phases without the corresponding energy, so the frictional dissipation was lost (Houim~2: $-0.55\%$). Each total-energy phase now receives $\Delta M\cdot U_I$ with $U_I$ the update-averaged particle velocity, as in the explicit drag update. (ii) The particles take the pressure force $-\alpha_s\nabla p$, but the fluid energy equations did not supply the matching work: in an expanding flow the pressure work on the particle-occupied volume, $\alpha_s p\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$, was created (up to $+2.8\%$ in the spherical charge). The fluids now receive together $-\nabla\cdot(\alpha_s p\,\mathbf{u}_s) + \alpha_s\mathbf{u}_s\cdot\nabla p$, shared by volume fraction, which closes the total-energy balance with the particle momentum equation. (iii) The implicit stage (viscous and conductive terms) rebuilt the conserved fields from the bounded primitive fields and re-applied the difference between the previous step's conserved and primitive fields, which removed energy wherever the decode bounds were active (up to $-1.2\%$). The implicit equations are now corrections to the current state with incremental updates of the conserved fields.
    \item \emph{Residual stabilisation.} The upstream fluid momentum and energy equations added their residual-cell stabilisation with the wrong sign, which lowered the matrix diagonal to $(\alpha\rho - \alpha_\text{res}\rho_\text{res})/\Delta t$: zero where $\alpha$ equals the residual fraction. This caused the floating-point exceptions of the 2D Comp-B/Al tutorial at 116 and 171\,$\mu$s; with the sign corrected the run reaches 200\,$\mu$s.
    \item \emph{Granular phase.} The particle packing limit is enforced by the frictional stress (with a small \texttt{alphaDeltaMin}), not by removing mass; the buoyancy on the particles uses the mixture fluid pressure, since the individual fluid pressures are undefined where a phase is absent.
\end{itemize}

The remaining known defects at this level are a few time steps in which trace gas ($\alpha \sim 10^{-6}$) inside the products reaches the 50\,000\,K decode cap, which is energetically negligible but not understood, (Section~\ref{sec:status}).

\subsection{Charges above the ground: 1D/2D simulation and AMR}
\label{sec:mapping}

A charge above the ground is simulated in two stages. Until the shock reaches the ground the flow is spherically symmetric and is computed in 1D on a graded mesh (0.5\,mm at the centre, 15\,mm at 4\,m), resolving the detonation, the breakout and the early fireball. Before the shock reaches the ground (at 75--90\% of the height of burst: 15\,$\mu$s, 0.1, 0.4 and 1.5\,ms for heights of 0.15, 0.5, 1 and 2\,m) the 1D solution is mapped onto a 2D axisymmetric mesh (a 5$^\circ$ wedge about the vertical axis through the charge; for the FOI cases $7.04\times5.12$\,m with 32\,mm base cells). The ground is a rigid, inviscid plane (symmetry condition), the side and the top are transmissive.

\paragraph{Mapping.} The fields of the 1D solution are interpolated onto the 2D mesh about the charge centre with the mesh refined around the charge at the same time (\texttt{rotateFields -refine -extend}). In parallel this must be done after the decomposition: \texttt{decomposePar} does not decompose the refinement history, so cells refined before it can never be coarsened. The mapped run counts as a restart (\texttt{beginTime 0}), otherwise the activation model would add the detonation energy a second time. All fields of the 1D case are mapped, including the particle diameter, the ignited fraction and the ignition progress; the physical models are those of the 1D case. Load balancing (\texttt{redistributor}) needs an empty \texttt{internal} patch in the mesh for the faces it exposes.

\paragraph{Refinement.} The refinement criteria are those of the 2D Comp-B/Al cases (Section~\ref{sec:res_g2}): 20\% jumps of the density across a face, the products/air mixing layer (marked by the carbon/(carbon + nitrogen) element ratio of the gas, 0.667 in the TNT products), and, for the weak shocks of the far field, absolute pressure jumps of more than \texttt{pressureDelta} = 2\,kPa per face, coarsened below 1\,kPa. Relative pressure jumps alone do not flag the shock beyond about 2.5\,m (a 20--30\,kPa shock smeared over 2--3 cells), which then runs on the 32\,mm base cells and loses up to 20\% of its peak. The finest level at the shock follows the 1D cell size at the shock radius through a table of the 1D arrival times, but is not coarser than \texttt{minLevel}; the FOI runs use level 3 (4\,mm) at the shock and level 2 (8\,mm) in the mixing layer. In a resolution study for FOI shot 4 (8, 4 and 2\,mm at the shock) the ground peaks at 4 and 6\,m change by 4.5\% from 8 to 4\,mm and by 0.5 and 3.9\% from 4 to 2\,mm, with the impulse unchanged. With the 2\,kPa criterion the free-field peaks at 2.5 and 3\,m are within 3\% of the 1D run. A 500\,Pa criterion sharpens the secondary shock (the largest rise in 30\,$\mu$s at 4\,m from 0.8 to 4.1\,kPa) and raises the main-shock peaks by 3--4\%, at 2.6 times the core-hours (15.7\,h on 96 processes against 9\,h on 64).

\paragraph{Cost.} The 1D stage of a FOI shot (radial slabs on 16 processes; 8 processes ran 3.4 times faster than 1) takes 41\,min to 5\,ms. The 2D stage from 1.5 to 18\,ms takes about 8\,h on 192 processes, or 8\,h for two runs of 96 processes on one node; 1D runs to 16\,ms on a 7\,m mesh, used for parameter studies with free-field probes at the gauge distances, take 3.5--4\,h on 16 processes.

\section{Verification Approach}
\label{sec:verification}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Verification and validation}

\emph{Verification} checks that the equations are solved correctly: conservation, agreement with exact or reference solutions, and agreement with another code solving the same model. \emph{Validation} checks that the model describes reality, by comparison with experiments. Most of the work is verification. The comparisons with empirical data are with the Kinney--Graham and Kingery--Bulmash correlations for TNT (Sections~\ref{sec:res_kb} and~\ref{sec:res_e1}) and with the FOI 2013 trials of TNT and TNT/Al charges (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}).

\subsection{Validation data}
\label{sec:datasets}

A dataset for the aluminised charge needs the charge composition (aluminium fraction and particle size), mass, shape and initiation; side-on or ground pressure histories at several distances in the range $Z \approx 0.5$--5\,m/kg$^{1/3}$, where the afterburning acts; and ideally a reference shot without aluminium (or with inert particles) from the same series, so that the aluminium contribution is measured directly, and the size and temperature of the fireball. No such data were found for Comp-B/Al. The candidates (survey in \path{report/h0_dataset_survey.md}) are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{FOI 2013} \cite{fedina2014,fedina2017}: about 1\,kg TNT and TNT/Al 80/20 spheres (42\,$\mu$m aluminium) at heights of burst of 0.15, 0.5, 1 and 2\,m, each with a TNT and a TNT/Al shot (TNT at 0.5\,m twice), ground gauges at 2, 4 and 6\,m (1\,MHz raw data available), a pyrometer and high-speed video. Pairs at the same height isolate the aluminium effect (15--27\% more impulse at 4--6\,m at every height, 130--330$^\circ$C higher fireball temperature), and the TNT repeat (shots 2 and 9) scatters by 3\% in impulse and 10--11\% in peak at 4 and 6\,m. The gauges are on the ground, so the trials need the 2D case with the ground (Section~\ref{sec:mapping}). This is the primary dataset.
    \item \emph{Pontalier et al.} \cite{pontalier2021}: gelled nitromethane in about 1\,L glass spheres with 10--50\% aluminium (13, 54 and 114\,$\mu$m), magnesium or inert glass beads, central C-4 booster, 1.52\,m above the ground, free field at the gauges until the ground reflection. Arrival times at 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0\,m and peaks at 2\,m for 20 single shots; the inert-glass shots separate the momentum and heat sink of the particles from their burning. A 1D spherical case of these trials exists (\path{tutorials/blastEulerFoam/Pontalier_NM_1D_spherical}) but has not yet been run with the current aluminium model.
    \item \emph{Trzci\'nski et al.} \cite{trzcinski2007}: RDX/wax with 15--60\% aluminium, LiF (inert) and TNT, 268\,g cylinders in a bunker, one side-on gauge at 2\,m; values only in figures. A secondary check, closest to the Comp-B/Al composition.
    \item TNT equivalences of H-6 and similar aluminised compositions (compiled free-air data): an integral check of a Comp-B/Al-like model.
\end{itemize}
Well-controlled repeat shots of plastic explosives scatter by $\pm2.5$\% in arrival and $\pm6$--8\% in peak and impulse \cite{farrimond2024}. The difference between the laminar and the PaSR limit of the afterburning (1--2\% in peak, about 5\% in impulse at 0.3--0.5\,m in 1D, Section~\ref{sec:res_g3c}) is therefore below the scatter, and the data test the aluminium effect and the overall energy release rather than the mixing closure. Arrival times are the most robust measured quantity.

\subsection{Test hierarchy and automation}

Each model is tested in the simplest configuration that isolates it, from 0D reactors (one cell, no transport) through 1D planar problems to 1D spherical blasts; multi-dimensional cases follow. Every test has a verification script with explicit pass criteria. The runner \path{bin/run_blast_tests.py} \tw{} runs the 18 tests in four tiers (Table~\ref{tab:tests}), checks the solver logs for errors and for normal completion, and runs the scripts. Some tests depend on external builds (\texttt{EuDetCode}, \texttt{gunIB1D}).

Three limitations of the current verification should be noted. First, the programmed burn imposes the detonation velocity, so agreement of the front speed with $D_\text{CJ}$ only checks the setup. Second, no mesh-convergence studies have been made; the reported values are for one mesh per case. Third, several tests check reference models outside the solver (the aluminium and high-temperature air models) and are marked as such.

\subsection{0D: transfer of products to gas}
\label{sec:res_0d}

A single closed cell is initialised at the matching state (100\,bar, $\rho = 35.234\,\text{kg/m}^3$, 836.10\,K) with eJWLC products, which transfer to the multicomponent gas with $\tau = 20\,\mu\text{s}$; the three-phase variant adds inert aluminium (Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_phase_transfer}). After 99.4\% of the products mass has transferred, the total energy is conserved to $4.2\times10^{-9}$ ($2.2\times10^{-9}$ with aluminium), the mass to $2.4\times10^{-9}$, and the net momentum is zero. The gas forms at the products temperature (difference $<10^{-3}\,\text{K}$) and the pressure stays constant to $3\times10^{-7}$. This confirms the conservation of the transfer and the datum reconciliation of Section~\ref{sec:reconciliation}. With the earlier datum convention ($\int c_v\,\mathrm{d}T$ from 298.15\,K) the pressure rose by 0.58\%; with the conservative decode not yet in place, the reset of the dilute products removed 1.8\% of the energy. The isobaric variant of the test planned in Stage~1 has not been made.

\subsection{1D canonical problems}
\label{sec:res_1d}

\paragraph{Shock in air.} A driver at 1700\,bar and 7500\,K drives a shock into air at 1\,atm and 300\,K (400 cells, 1\,m, \texttt{blastFoam}, HLLC). The air is N$_2$, O$_2$, NO, N and O with NASA-9 thermodynamics (perfect gas) and local chemical equilibrium above 2500\,K, with no finite-rate reactions. The shock moves at 4839\,m/s (Mach 13.9), measured by a fit of the sub-cell shock position over the last three writes, and the solver gives $p_2 = 244\,\text{bar}$, $T_2 = 6626\,\text{K}$, $\rho_2 = 10.95\,\text{kg/m}^3$ and $u_2 = 4310\,\text{m/s}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stage2_high_mach}). At the measured shock speed the exact jump for the same species data in chemical equilibrium (computed independently of the solver) gives $p_2 = 247\,\text{bar}$, $T_2 = 6603\,\text{K}$, $\rho_2 = 11.15\,\text{kg/m}^3$ and $u_2 = 4330\,\text{m/s}$: the solver is within 1.2\%, 0.35\%, 1.7\% and 0.46\%, and its post-shock composition ($Y_\text{O} = 0.162$, $Y_\text{NO} = 0.096$, $Y_\text{N} = 0.017$, $Y_{\text{O}_2} = 0.019$) is within 0.0011 of the equilibrium values. Without the chemistry (frozen air) the same shock would give $T_2 = 8990\,\text{K}$ and $\rho_2 = 9.30\,\text{kg/m}^3$; before this work, with the JANAF air extrapolated without dissociation, the solver gave about 10\,000\,K at Mach 14.5. The driver gas is also in equilibrium, which weakens the shock from Mach 14.5 to 13.9.

\paragraph{Contact advection.} A density contact ($116$ / $16\,\text{kg/m}^3$) advected at 500\,m/s and 100\,bar stays within one cell of its exact position for HLLC, AUSM+, Kurganov and Tadmor (Figure~\ref{fig:stage2_multiphase_advection}). Spurious pressure oscillations at the contact are 4.0--4.4\% with the van Leer limiter and below 0.8\% with Minmod, which is why Minmod is used; the target of $10^{-6}$ is not met, as expected of a scheme that is not designed to be pressure-equilibrium-preserving.

\paragraph{Granular shock problems.} Three problems of Houim and Oran type (advection of a particle curtain, a shock passing through a particle bed, and compaction of a dense bed) are solved with \texttt{blastEulerFoam} and with \texttt{gunIB1D} (Figure~\ref{fig:stage2_gunib1d}). The curtain advection is identical; for the shock tube the transmitted shock speed differs by 1.20\% (489.2 vs 495.1\,m/s), the post-shock pressure by 0.62\%, the gas velocity by 0.25\% and the particle velocity by 0.32\% (2.1\% before the drag energy fix of Section~\ref{sec:multiphase_numerics}); the total energy is conserved to $6\times10^{-7}$; for the compaction problem the peak pressure differs by 0.59\%, and the compaction wave speed of \texttt{blastEulerFoam} (183.8\,m/s) is within 0.8\% of the reference value. The compaction result required the frictional stress to act (\texttt{alphaDeltaMin} $10^{-6}$); with the upstream tutorial value the bed packed to the maximum fraction and the old decode removed 41\% of the particle mass.

\paragraph{Planar detonation.} A planar Comp-B charge (without aluminium, 1717\,kg/m$^3$) is initiated at a closed end (\texttt{blastFoam}, 400 cells, 0.5\,mm, SSP Runge--Kutta (RK2SSP) time integration at a Courant number of 0.35). The products EOS is the \texttt{EuDetCode} JWL fit for Comp-B (BKW), with $E_0$ chosen so that the reference isentrope passes through the CJ point for the reactant energy of the case; the CJ point of this products EOS (the minimum speed on its Hugoniot) is then the programmed-burn speed, 7934.3\,m/s. The pressure at the burn front stays at 22.8--24.9\,GPa ($p_\text{CJ} = 24.66$\,GPa) and the solution behind follows the Taylor expansion (Figure~\ref{fig:stage2_planar_znd}). The earlier setup used the LLNL Comp-B JWL with $E_0 = -8.5$\,MJ/kg (the LLNL value is 8.5\,GJ/m$^3$, i.e.\ 4.94\,MJ/kg) and the \texttt{EuDetCode} burn speed of 7947\,m/s: the CJ speed of that products EOS is 8403\,m/s, so no steady detonation exists at the imposed speed, the energy released behind the front overdrives it, and the pressure at the front grew to 55, 95 and 135\,GPa at 5, 10 and 15\,$\mu$s. The test now checks the consistency of the products EOS with the burn speed and the front pressure. The variant with \texttt{pressurePhaseChange} converts the products to gas below 100\,bar without disturbing the wave.

\subsection{Spherical blast of a C4 charge}
\label{sec:res_kb}

A 1\,kg spherical C4 charge (JWL, programmed burn at the CJ speed of the JWL, 8227.6\,m/s; \texttt{blastFoam}) is detonated at the centre of a 1D spherical domain of 6\,m with 300 cells ($\Delta r = 20\,\text{mm}$), and the peak overpressure and positive impulse are recorded in every cell with \texttt{temporalMax} and \texttt{temporalIntegral} (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_spherical_blast}). The reference is the Kinney--Graham correlation for TNT \cite{kinney} (the verification script is named after Kingery--Bulmash \cite{kingery}, but evaluates the Kinney--Graham formulas; the C4 mass is used without TNT equivalence). The peak overpressure is low at all scaled distances: 36.2 vs 40.0\,bar at $Z = 0.5\,\text{m/kg}^{1/3}$, 8.5 vs 10.1\,bar at $Z = 1$, 1.71 vs 2.08\,bar at $Z = 2$ and 0.75 vs 0.82\,bar at $Z = 3$ (9--20\%). The positive impulse differs by 28\%, 32\%, 2\%, 1\% and 9\% at $Z = 0.5$, 1, 1.5, 2 and 3 (until 2026-10-15 the script's Kinney--Graham impulse integrated a Friedlander pulse with an assumed decay parameter, which underestimated the far-field impulse by a factor of up to 3; it now uses the published closed form). (With the earlier burn speed of 7850\,m/s, below the CJ speed of the JWL, the peaks were about 1\% lower.) Since C4 is more energetic than TNT (TNT equivalence about 1.2--1.4), the simulated overpressures would be expected to lie above the TNT curve; the low peaks therefore point to the resolution (20\,mm cells smear the shock) and to the JWL parameters of the case. A mesh-convergence study and a TNT or Comp-B case are needed before the 5\% target of the plan can be assessed.

\subsection{Spherical TNT and Comp-B charges against Kingery--Bulmash}
\label{sec:res_e1}

Plan step E1 compares 1\,kg spherical charges of TNT and of aluminium-free Comp-B with the Kingery--Bulmash data for TNT \cite{kingery} (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_blast_kb}). The cases (\path{tests/1D_spherical/tnt_blast}, \path{compB_blast}; \texttt{blastFoam}, 1D spherical sector of 10\,m, centre-initiated programmed burn at the CJ speed of the products EOS, ideal-gas air at 101\,325\,Pa and 288.15\,K, no afterburning) use \texttt{EuDetCode} BKW products: TNT at 1630\,kg/m$^3$ ($D$ = 6696\,m/s, $p_\text{CJ}$ = 16.9\,GPa; \path{utilities/thermo_fitter/eudet_tnt}) and the Comp-B of the planar test. The reference (\path{tests/1D_spherical/kingery_bulmash.py}) is the Kingery--Bulmash free-air overpressure polynomial; for the impulse and the arrival time, for which only the hemispherical fits of Swisdak \cite{swisdak} were available, the hemispherical value at the charge mass that reproduces the free-air overpressure at the same scaled distance is used (a mass ratio of 1.3--2.1, instead of the usual constant reflection factor 1.8, which would shift the overpressure by $-13$ to $+7\%$). The Swisdak coefficients reproduce the upstream validation data and the examples of IATG 01.80 within 1\%. Comp-B is compared through the TNT equivalence of UFC 3-340-02 (1.11 for pressure, 0.98 for impulse).

The arrival times agree within 1.3\% for $Z \ge 2\,\text{m/kg}^{1/3}$. The peak overpressure depends on the mesh, and on the resolution of the charge more than of the air: for TNT at $Z = 8$ it is 5.6, 4.1 and 0.5\% below the reference with 4, 2 and 1\,mm cells, and 1.4\% below with 1\,mm cells only to 0.3\,m (2\,mm beyond); the limiter (Minmod or van Leer) changes it by less than 0.5\%. At 1\,mm the TNT peak overpressure is within 3.4\% of the reference for $Z \ge 4$ and 5--8\% low at $Z$ = 1.5--3; Comp-B at 2\,mm is within 4\% for $Z \ge 4$ and within 8\% for $Z$ = 1--3. Gate 3 (5\% for $Z \ge 1$) is therefore met in the far field but not at $Z$ = 1.5--3, where also the reference is least certain. The positive impulse does not depend on the mesh and is low: for TNT 14--17\% at $Z$ = 4--8 (Kinney--Graham \cite{kinney} gives similar far-field values), for Comp-B 5--9\%. Since the overpressure agrees there, the deficit is a short positive phase (TNT: 13--23\% shorter than the reference at 4--8\,m); the larger deficit for TNT, whose products are much more oxygen-deficient (oxygen balance $-74\%$ against $-43\%$ for Comp-B), points to the afterburning of the products in air, which is part of the experiments but not of these cases. The tests run on the 4\,mm mesh (8--13\,min) with regression bounds (arrival 3\% for $Z \ge 3$, overpressure 20\% and impulse 25\%).

\subsection{Spherical Comp-B/Al charge}
\label{sec:res_spherical}

A spherical Comp-B/Al charge of radius 50\,mm (0.95\,kg) is initiated at its centre in a 1D spherical domain of 0.2\,m with 400 cells ($\Delta r = 0.5\,\text{mm}$) and run to 40\,$\mu$s with \texttt{blastEulerFoam}. Three variants share the thermodynamics: inert aluminium, the same with gas-phase chemistry, and the same with aluminium burning (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_spherical_multiphase}).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Detonation.} The front moves at 7149\,m/s, as imposed. The peak pressure grows to 15.2\,GPa near the charge edge, 76\% of $p_\text{CJ}$; the CJ spike is not resolved on this mesh, and the effect of resolution has not been quantified.
    \item \emph{Breakout and air shock.} In the reacting cases (NASA-9 gas in local equilibrium above 2500\,K) the pressure drops to 2.81\,GPa at the charge/air interface, and the air shock is 37\,MPa at 80\,mm, 28\,MPa at 100\,mm (arrival 14.9\,$\mu$s) and 18\,MPa at 150\,mm (with the plain HLLC flux 2.94\,GPa and 36, 27 and 17\,MPa; with 1\,mm instead of 30\,$\mu$m particles, which take less momentum and heat from the products, 42, 32 and 21\,MPa); with the earlier JANAF gas without dissociation it was 45, 34 and 21\,MPa. These values changed substantially during the work (e.g.\ 0.16\,GPa at 100\,mm before the density floor was removed, 29\,MPa before the constant-$c_p$ extrapolation, 35\,MPa before the energy fixes below), which shows the sensitivity of the near field to the treatment of trace phases, high-temperature air and energy conservation. With dissociation the shocked air reaches at most 9300\,K (at 10\,$\mu$s), against 16\,500\,K without it. The first mixed cell at the charge/air interface still reaches the 50\,000\,K decode cap during breakout (6.9--7.2\,$\mu$s): its gas stays at ambient density while the products are at 0.7\,GPa, a pressure non-equilibrium of the mixed cell (Section~\ref{sec:status}), not of the chemistry.
    \item \emph{Aluminium.} All variants use 30\,$\mu$m particles; the Al-combustion variant uses the aluminium models of Section~\ref{sec:al_impl} (melting, Loth drag, Kavanau heat transfer, Beckstead burning to oxide with the ignited fraction, $T_\text{ign}$ = 2050\,K). Drag by the products accelerates the aluminium to up to 3300--3400\,m/s where $\alpha_\text{Al} > 10^{-3}$ and the heat transfer melts it: by 40\,$\mu$s the mass-mean temperature is 1550--1700\,K, and 59\% of the aluminium is ignited in the Al-combustion variant. The particle temperature is not limited by vaporisation and reaches 3500--5700\,K in a few cells at the edge of the cloud, in the shocked air. Particles surrounded by products only do not burn; at 40\,$\mu$s the burning has just started at the edge of the cloud ($3\times10^{-5}$ of the aluminium mass). The leading edge of the cloud reaches the boundary of the 0.2\,m domain at about 36\,$\mu$s, before the air shock.
    \item \emph{Products afterburning.} In the reacting and Al-combustion variants the products are transferred to the gas below 100\,bar ($\tau = 20\,\mu$s). By 35\,$\mu$s 12.4\% of the products mass has been transferred; the carbon in the gas is then still distributed as in the transferred products (53\% CO, 18\% CO$_2$, 9\% CH$_4$, 20\% C(gr)), since in 1D the transferred products and the air hardly mix in this time. The blast pressures to 40\,$\mu$s are unchanged by the transfer, which happens behind the shock; the afterburning becomes significant only on longer times and larger domains.
    \item \emph{Interface.} A separate case with an unreacted charge next to air stays at rest: velocities below $10^{-3}\,\text{m/s}$ at 5\,$\mu$s and below 1.4\,mm/s for 40\,$\mu$s, pressure at 1.013\,bar, mass and energy conserved to $1.5\times10^{-9}$. Before the trace-phase changes of Section~\ref{sec:multiphase_numerics} this case became unstable within microseconds.
\end{itemize}

These cases show that the detonation, breakout and early air shock of a three-phase charge run stably. While the domain is closed (after the burn, before the shock leaves), the total energy of the three phases, including the kinetic and granular energy of the Al, is conserved to $3.4\times10^{-4}$ (inert) and $3.8\times10^{-4}$ (with transfer and chemistry, counting the formation enthalpy of the gas). The remaining loss, about $10^{-5}$ per $\mu$s, appears with the 30\,$\mu$m particles, which the products accelerate and heat strongly (with 1\,mm particles it was $5\times10^{-5}$); it is about 0.3\% of the energy exchanged with the particles and is not yet explained. The products transfer adds about $2\times10^{-5}$: until the species fluxes were built from the phase mass flux, the normalisation of the mass fractions moved mass between the species and the balance drifted by $-1.4\times10^{-3}$, and the Houim~2 gas/particle shock tube to $6\times10^{-7}$; the tests check this. Before the fixes of Section~\ref{sec:multiphase_numerics} the spherical cases gained up to 2.8\% and then lost 1.2\%, and Houim~2 lost 0.55\%. The products transfer adds a mass error of up to $5\times10^{-6}$ (transfer into cells where the gas is still residual). They do not yet test the afterburning, which needs the products transfer, the aluminium ignition and combustion models, a domain of metres and run times of milliseconds.

\subsection{Inert and reacting Comp-B/Al charge on a 2\,m domain}
\label{sec:res_e2}

Plan step E2 compares the inert and the reacting Comp-B/Al charge on the 2\,m domain of \path{tutorials/blastEulerFoam/ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_2m} (1D spherical, 0.5\,mm cells to 0.1\,m, graded to 10\,mm at 2\,m, run to 2\,ms; \path{setup_e2.py}, \path{compare_e2.py}). All runs use the aluminium models of Section~\ref{sec:al_impl}: melting, Loth drag and Kavanau heat transfer for the 30\,$\mu$m particles in the gas, Beckstead burning to oxide with the gas-phase oxidisers and the transported ignited fraction, and the gas phase with the aluminium sub-oxides and liquid Al$_2$O$_3$. The aluminium exchanges momentum and heat with the products as well as with the gas (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}); within 20\,$\mu$s the products melt all of it and heat it to a mass-mean 1800\,K. Five variants: inert (no products $\to$ gas transfer, no burning), products transfer without aluminium burning, and products transfer with aluminium ignition at three temperatures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $T_\text{ign} = 2050$\,K, the melting point of the oxide, for particles with an intact oxide shell;
    \item $T_\text{ign} = 932.5$\,K, the onset of aluminium melting, for a shell ruptured by the detonation, so that ignition is set by the aluminium core. The core is about 3.5 times more compressible than the oxide; at the 10--25\,GPa of the detonation the mismatch of the two is 1.7--4.4\% in linear strain, and the melting of the core adds 2\% (6.5\% in volume), each far beyond the strain a thin alumina film sustains (stresses of several GPa against a strength of about 1\,GPa);
    \item $T_\text{ign} = 0$: burning from the start, a bound.
\end{itemize}

The products transfer and their afterburning add 2--5\% to the peak overpressure and 3--7\% to the impulse beyond 0.3\,m (Table~\ref{tab:e2}). The aluminium adds much more: 10\% to the peak at 0.5\,m and 36--52\% from 1\,m outwards, and doubles the impulse at 1\,m, because it releases about 31\,MJ/kg against about 5\,MJ/kg of explosive. About 90--95\% of the burnt aluminium burns with the oxygen of the air and 5--10\% with the H$_2$O and CO$_2$ of the transferred products, and the oxidiser consumed matches the burnt aluminium within 1.5\%. Most of it burns in the shocked air at 1--1.5\,m, where the 30\,$\mu$m particles have run ahead of the products (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_e2_ignition}).

The ignition temperature matters much less (Figure~\ref{fig:stage3_e2_ignition}). With 932.5\,K and with 0 the runs are identical, since the products melt all the aluminium within 20\,$\mu$s; with a ruptured shell the aluminium ignites in effect as soon as it is in contact with gas-phase oxidiser. With 2050\,K, 37\% of the aluminium is ignited by 20\,$\mu$s (hot products near the charge) and 99\% by 140\,$\mu$s, as the cloud passes through the hot shocked and afterburning gas (particle temperatures up to 3550\,K). The burnt fractions are the same to 0.5\,ms (24\%) and differ only later, in the dilute outer cloud, where part of the aluminium is never heated to 2050\,K: 49 against 51\% at 1\,ms and 66.5 against 80.4\% at 1.9\,ms. The peak overpressure differs by less than 1\%, and the impulse at 1\,m by 13 percentage points (positive phase not yet complete). For this charge and particle size the ignition criterion therefore changes the late burnt fraction but hardly the blast; the oxygen available to the particles and their burn rate decide it.

These are 1D results, and the aluminium contribution is probably overestimated: the particles penetrate the shocked air without the mixing limit of a multi-dimensional fireball, burn there at the Beckstead rate for quiescent particles, and the particle temperature is not limited by vaporisation (it reaches 3550\,K, above the boiling point of 2790\,K). After 1.9\,ms the remaining cloud is so dilute ($\alpha_\text{Al} \lesssim 2\times10^{-5}$) that cells fall below the residual volume fraction ($10^{-6}$), where the particle energy and the ignited fraction are reset. Beyond 1.9\,m the stronger blast pushes air out of the domain. The transfer settings matter little: with 200\,bar and 20\,$\mu$s instead of 100\,bar the gauges change by at most 0.3\%; a fast transfer (100\,bar, 2\,$\mu$s) changes the peaks by up to 2.3\% and the impulse at 0.3\,m by 10\%.

\subsection{2D axisymmetric spherical Comp-B/Al charge}
\label{sec:res_g1}

The inert spherical case of Section~\ref{sec:res_spherical} is repeated on a 2D axisymmetric mesh (\path{tests/2D_axi/ChargeCompBAl_2D_axi_spherical}, plan step G1): a 5$^\circ$ wedge of the quarter plane of 0.2 $\times$ 0.2\,m with a symmetry plane through the charge centre. The base cells of 2\,mm are refined by AMR in two levels to the 0.5\,mm of the 1D case, at the density gradient, pressure jumps and the edge of the Al cloud (the charge from the start), with load balancing on 6 processes. Pressure probes are placed every 5$^\circ$ at 0.10 and 0.15\,m and on five rays at the radii of the 1D probes.

With the plain HLLC flux the air shock is spherical between 10 and 80$^\circ$ to 3\% in peak and 1.5\% in arrival, but not along the axis and the symmetry plane (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g1_axisymmetric}): on the axis the peak is 6--9\% low and the shock 1--2\% early, and from 14\,$\mu$s a trace of products runs along both up to the shock (7\,mm ahead of the contact elsewhere at 30\,$\mu$s). The same happens on a uniform 0.5\,mm mesh without AMR, so it is a carbuncle of the HLLC flux at the grid-aligned shock, not an effect of the refinement. With the hybrid flux (Section~\ref{sec:discretisation}) the axis and plane values lie within the scatter of the other directions, the contact is at the same radius in all directions (150.5--152.0\,mm at 26\,$\mu$s) and the shock radius varies by 0.45\%.

Against the 1D case with the same flux, the mean of the 30--60$^\circ$ rays arrives 0.8--1.2\% early and has 2.6--4.3\% higher peaks at 80--150\,mm (HLLC: 0.3--0.7\% and 0.9--2.7\%). The 1D case itself changes by up to 1.4\% in arrival and 2.8\% in peak from 0.5 to 0.25\,mm, so the 2D and 1D results agree to within the resolution sensitivity, and the plan target of an angular variation below 0.5\% is not reachable at 0.5\,mm. AMR gives the same probe results as the uniform mesh to 0.3\% in 2.2 instead of 5.7\,h (38\%), although by 40\,$\mu$s the refined region covers 85\% of this small domain.

\subsection{2D axisymmetric Comp-B/Al charge to 2\,m}
\label{sec:res_g2}

The full 2\,m case of Section~\ref{sec:res_e2} (\texttt{nasa9} gas with 28 species, aluminium burning, ignition at 2050\,K, hybrid flux) is run in 2D to 2\,ms (\path{tutorials/blastEulerFoam/ChargeCompBAl_2D_axi_2m}, plan step G2). The mesh is a 5$^\circ$ wedge of the quarter plane of 2 $\times$ 2\,m with base cells of 16\,mm. Until 60\,$\mu$s the flow is spherically symmetric and needs 0.5\,mm cells for the detonation and the breakout, so the 1D solution at 60\,$\mu$s is mapped onto the 2D mesh (\texttt{rotateFields} with refinement, in parallel after \texttt{decomposePar}, which does not carry the refinement history; otherwise the mapped cells can never be coarsened). The mesh is refined where neighbouring cells differ by more than 20\% in density or pressure and at the edge of the aluminium cloud (\texttt{scaledDelta}). The finest level decreases with time like the cell size of the 1D graded mesh at the shock radius: 2\,mm to 350\,$\mu$s, 4\,mm to 1.27\,ms, then 8\,mm. A density-gradient criterion kept the whole fireball refined (74\,000 cells at 60\,$\mu$s). Three faults were found and fixed on the way: cells above a decreasing maximum level were not coarsened, the maximum level of several estimators was that of the last one, and on a restart the detonation energy was added to the reacted material a second time (1D restart at 60\,$\mu$s: core pressure 820 instead of 122\,bar before the fix; the restarted run agrees with the continuous one within $5\times10^{-4}$ at 80\,$\mu$s).

The run takes 5.6\,h on 6 processes (6875 steps, 17\,700--24\,900 cells; Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g2_blast}f). A uniform mesh at the same finest cell size in each interval would need 27 times the cell-steps, so AMR saves 96\% (G2 target 75\%).

Against the 1D case on the rays at 0, 45 and 90$^\circ$ between 0.3 and 1.9\,m (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g2_blast}a--c), the shock arrives within 1.1\%, the peak overpressure is 1--9\% higher (one point 0.7\% lower) and the positive impulse 1--27\% higher, most at 0.5\,m (18--27\%). Over the angle the peaks vary by 1.1, 4.2, 3.7 and 5.4\% at 0.3, 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5\,m (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g2_blast}d), highest at 30--45$^\circ$ and lowest at the axis and the symmetry plane beyond 0.3\,m, i.e. with the orientation of the Cartesian cells; the carbuncle of Section~\ref{sec:res_g1} does not reappear. The aluminium burns as in 1D (60 and 57\% at 2\,ms), but 14\% less CO (and 12\% less H$_2$) remains in 2D (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g2_blast}e), mostly burnt between 0.1 and 0.5\,ms; about 0.03\,kg more CO is oxidised than in 1D, of the order of 0.3\,MJ, consistent with the higher peaks and impulse.

The additional afterburning is numerical mixing, not resolved instability growth (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g2_fields}). The products/air mixing layer (10--90\% of the CO mass fraction) is 41--64\,mm wide in 2D at 0.2\,ms and 89--128\,mm at 0.5\,ms, against 12 and 28\,mm in 1D, and widest along the axis and the symmetry plane. From 0.5\,ms all cells of the mixing layer are at the base level (16\,mm): once the layer is spread over several cells, the jump between neighbouring cells is below the 20\% of the refinement criterion, so the layer is no longer refined and diffuses further. The fireball shows no instability structure at this resolution. The 2D results therefore do not yet show the effect of multi-dimensional mixing: the refinement has to follow the mixing layer (a criterion on the products or species fractions, or on the gradient across several cells) at a resolution comparable to the 1D case before the mixing studies of G3.

\paragraph{Mixing layer refined (G2b).} The mixing layer is marked by the ratio of the carbon to the carbon and nitrogen element mass fractions of the gas, which is 0.449 in the products and zero in air, is conserved by the gas chemistry and is not changed by the aluminium oxides (the carbon fraction alone drops by 2\% where the aluminium burns). A new error estimator (\texttt{linearCombination}: a weighted sum of fields, optionally divided by a second one) refines the cells with a ratio between 0.01 and 0.44 to a fixed 2\,mm, about the cell size of the 1D mesh at the fireball edge; the other criteria are unchanged. Run to 1.3\,ms, the layer is 12--17\,mm wide at 0.1\,ms (1D: 13\,mm) and 45--72\,mm at 1.3\,ms (1D: 151\,mm, diffused on its 3.5\,mm cells). The CO and H$_2$ burnout is now within 1\% of 1D, the peaks within $-3.1$ to $+4.4$\% and the positive impulse at 0.3 and 0.5\,m within $-4$ to $+6.5$\% (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g2b_mixing}); the arrival is within 2.5\%. The higher peaks and impulse of the G2 run were therefore the numerical mixing. The run takes 18\,h on 6 processes (up to 71\,000 cells; the 2\,mm layer limits the time step), 5\% of the cell-steps of a uniform 2\,mm mesh.

From 0.5\,ms the fireball surface forms Rayleigh--Taylor fingers about 50--60\,mm apart (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g2b_mixing}d--f). They grow from the mesh, so their wavelength and growth are not converged; up to 1.3\,ms they do not change the CO burnout. Along the axis and the symmetry plane the inward secondary shock runs ahead: at 0.5\,ms the gas at 0.5--0.6\,m moves inwards at 460--500\,m/s there, against 1000\,m/s outwards in the other directions. Air then runs into the fireball along the axis, and a jet of products runs out along the symmetry plane to 0.8\,m. The cause is the Cartesian mesh (below).

\paragraph{Axis.} The axis behaviour was traced on the inert spherical case of Section~\ref{sec:res_g1} on a 0.6\,m wedge with uniform cells (no AMR), where the gas and the aluminium next to the axis gain a velocity away from the axis that grows to 2\% (gas) and 4.4\% (aluminium) of the radial velocity by 0.15\,ms on 4\,mm cells, against at most 0.3\% at 30--60$^\circ$ (single-phase \texttt{blastFoam} on the same mesh: 0.4\%). Neither the HLLC/HLL flux, the cells at the axis (a cut-out axis patch), the gradient scheme of the buoyancy $\alpha_\mathrm{s}\nabla p$ of the particles, the particle flux (HLL) nor a first-order particle velocity changes it. In the first 20\,$\mu$s the shock and the aluminium cloud turn by a few per cent towards the directions aligned with the mesh, the axis and the symmetry plane alike; on the axis the inflow concentrates in the small cells there, so that by 20\,$\mu$s the aluminium fraction next to the axis is 18\% above that next to the plane, and the pressure there later drives the flow away from the axis. The pressureless particles carry the error and the drag transfers it to the gas (without drag the gas error is below 0.5\%, but that run is unstable in the detonation). In the cell column at the axis the error is first order in the cell size (4.6, 2.1 and 1.1\% at 0.1\,ms on 8, 4 and 2\,mm; Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_axis_mesh}c), but within about 25$^\circ$ of the axis the aluminium distribution does not converge: an excess within 15$^\circ$ of the axis on 4\,mm becomes a deficit with a ring of 40--50\% excess at 22--25$^\circ$ on 2\,mm (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_axis_mesh}b). These are grid-seeded perturbations focused by the axis geometry, which a sharp mixing layer (G2b) lets grow. The 0 and 90$^\circ$ rays of the 2D runs are therefore mesh-dependent, and the 2D results are compared with 1D away from them; the mixing studies of G3 need seeded perturbations larger than this grid noise or a mesh not aligned with the axis.

\label{sec:res_g3}

Plan step G3 (details in \path{report/stage5_g3_plan.md}) uses the 2D 2\,m case with the refined mixing layer (G2b) for three questions: the blast of cylindrical charges (G3a), the growth of a perturbed products/air contact (G3b), and the sensitivity of the afterburning to the turbulence and combustion closures (G3c).

\subsubsection{Cylindrical charges (G3a)}
\label{sec:res_g3a}

The case \path{tutorials/blastEulerFoam/ChargeCompBAl_2D_axi_cylinder} is the G2b tutorial with a cylindrical charge about the axis of the same volume and mass (0.953\,kg) as the sphere, initiated at its centre. The mid-plane is then a symmetry plane, and the quarter plane of the sphere case is kept. The charge is set and refined to 0.5\,mm after \texttt{decomposePar}, and the 2D detonation runs from $t = 0$ (no 1D mapping). Four faults appeared in the first runs and were fixed. The cell arrival times of the programmed burn were mapped between refinement levels and distorted the front (35\% ahead at 45$^\circ$ after 2.5\,$\mu$s); they are now recomputed from the mesh points after each mesh change. Along the grazing detonation, shocked air at 10--40\,kK heated traces of unreacted explosive to the temperature cap; the explosive/gas heat transfer is now scaled by the reaction progress $\lambda$, so only the reacted fraction equilibrates with the gas. Partly reacted explosive carried off the side of the charge expanded to 4--8\,kg/m$^3$, where the cold tension of its Murnaghan EOS drove it to the cap; it is now set fully reacted below half its initial density (\texttt{expansionBurnRatio}, energy conserved through $\lambda$). Finally, the equilibrium solver rejects non-finite or extreme states before its singular value decomposition, and the temperature solver uses a secant slope where the analytic $c_v$ of a trace gas near the covolume limit does not match $e(T)$. The runs advance by about 10\,$\mu$s per 30\,min on 6 cores with the 2\,mm mixing layer, so the L/D 1 and 4 runs were stopped at 267 and 173\,$\mu$s and parked for continuation on a larger machine.

Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g3a_cylinders} compares the peak overpressure and the arrival time with the 1D sphere of the same mass and physics, at the probes at least 5\,cm from the charge. The 2D sphere (G2b) is within 1.2\% of 1D at 0.3\,m and 4\% at 0.5\,m on all rays, the axis included, so the cylinder effects are well above the 2D error. For L/D 1 the end-on (axis) peak is 2.1--2.4 times that of the sphere at 0.3--0.5\,m, the side-on peak 1.4--1.6 times, and between them, at 30--60$^\circ$, only 0.50--0.64 times; the shock arrives there 15--37\% later than the sphere's, between the side and the end wave. For L/D 4 the end-on peak at 0.3\,m is 2.6 times the sphere's, side-on 1.04 (1.44 at 15$^\circ$ and 0.5\,m) and 0.52--0.59 at 45--60$^\circ$. With centre initiation the detonation runs along the axis into both end faces, which gives the strong end-on waves; at 0.3\,m the side-on peak of L/D 4 is below that of L/D 1.

The comparison with the literature uses the hydrocode studies of centre-initiated TNT cylinders of Gao et al.\ \cite{gao2022} and Xiao et al.\ \cite{xiao2020}, as the shape factor $S_p$ (peak over that of the sphere of equal mass) at the scaled distance $Z$ (0.30 and 0.51\,m/kg$^{1/3}$ at 0.3 and 0.5\,m with a TNT equivalence of 1). For L/D 4 the fit of Gao et al.\ (their Eq.~(9) with Table~2, valid for L/D $\ge 2$; \path{utilities/cylinder_blast_reference.py}, sphere from the Henrych fit) gives 0.81, 0.84, 0.90, 0.64, 0.49, 1.08 and 2.37 at 0, 15, \dots, 90$^\circ$ at 0.3\,m against 1.04, 1.16, 0.94, 0.52, 0.59, 0.81 and 2.55 in the run; at 0.5\,m 1.33, 1.25 and 2.45 at 15, 75 and 90$^\circ$ against 1.44, 0.65 and 2.16. For L/D 1, outside the fit, Gao et al.\ (their Fig.~15a) give 1.9--2.1 end-on, 1.15--1.3 side-on and 0.45--0.6 at the intermediate angles, and Xiao et al.\ (Fig.~8, $Z = 0.5$) 3.0--3.4 end-on and about 1.45 side-on. Both studies find the axial peak highest at small $Z$ for centre initiation (for $Z < 2.68$ at L/D 1 and $Z < 0.73$ at L/D 4 in Gao et al.), a deep minimum between the side and the end waves, and the radial peak of L/D 4 near or below the sphere at $Z = 0.3$, rising to its maximum (1.5--2) only at $Z \approx 0.75$--1.2. The lower side-on peak of L/D 4 than of L/D 1 at 0.3\,m is therefore what both studies predict at this distance; the rise of the side-on peak with L/D of Knock and Davies \cite{knock2013} comes from data at larger $Z$. The run lies within the spread of the two studies, which differ by up to 50\% on the axis at $Z = 0.5$; the largest offsets from the fit are $+0.23$ side-on at 0.3\,m (L/D 4) and the 75$^\circ$ ray at 0.5\,m (0.65 against 1.25), close to the axis where the fireball is mesh-dependent (Section~\ref{sec:res_g2}). The studies use TNT with ideal-gas air and no afterburning, $Z$ depends on the TNT equivalence assumed for Comp-B/Al, and only peaks are compared so far. The continued runs should show at 1.0\,m ($Z \approx 1$) a side-on peak of L/D 4 of 1.5--2 times the sphere's and above that of L/D 1, and an axis peak of L/D 4 falling below the sphere by $Z \approx 1.5$--2.

\subsubsection{Seeded products/air contact (G3b)}
\label{sec:res_g3b}

The G2b fingers grow from the grid, so their wavelength and growth are not a result. G3b seeds a single mode on the contact instead and measures its growth and its effect on the afterburning (\path{tutorials/blastEulerFoam/ChargeCompBAl_2D_axi_seeded}). \texttt{perturb\_contact.py} displaces the contact of the mapped 1D solution by $a_0\cos(n\psi)$ ($\psi$ the angle from the symmetry plane, $n$ even): in a band around the contact each cell takes the state of the 1D cell at the displaced radius. The diagnostics (\texttt{contact\_modes.py}) use the products fraction $f$ of each cell, from the carbon/(carbon + nitrogen) ratio of the gas (Section~\ref{sec:res_g2}) and the products phase. On rays every 0.5$^\circ$ in the window 22.5--67.5$^\circ$, clear of the axis and the symmetry plane, they give the contact radius $R(\psi)$ (the edge of the products core plus the products path length beyond it), the mixing width $W(\psi) = \int_{r > R/2} 4f(1-f)\,\mathrm{d}r$, the amplitudes $a_n$ of the modes over the window, and the CO and H$_2$ in the domain. $W$ is integrated over the outer half of each ray only: in G2b the air carried into the fireball by the products jet along the symmetry plane (about 2\,g at $\psi < 10^\circ$) reaches the centre, where all rays meet, after the reflection of the secondary shock, and the full-ray width of G2b jumps from 105 to 132\,mm between 1.2 and 1.3\,ms.

In the 1D solution the contact is Rayleigh--Taylor unstable from 60\,$\mu$s to 1.3\,ms: the denser products decelerate against the air, with an Atwood number from $-0.25$ (60--200\,$\mu$s) to $-0.05$ (1.3\,ms); the contact stops at 0.755\,m at 0.74\,ms and then moves inwards. In the window the unseeded G2b contact has a scatter of 1.7--2.4\,mm between rays to 0.2\,ms and 23--34\,mm at 1--1.3\,ms, with mode amplitudes $|a_{24}|$ and $|a_{48}|$ of 0.5--1\,mm to 0.5\,ms and about 6\,mm at 1.3\,ms (the grid noise). A test with $n = 24$ and 3\,mm seeded at 60\,$\mu$s, when the contact moves at 2--2.6\,km/s, lost 57\% of the amplitude by 100\,$\mu$s before it grew with an e-folding time of about 55\,$\mu$s (linear Rayleigh--Taylor theory: about 40\,$\mu$s); the seed carries no velocity perturbation, and the 2\,mm cells diffuse a fast-moving contact, and the two causes could not be separated. The run reported here therefore seeds later: $n = 24$ with 6\,mm at 200\,$\mu$s (5.4\,mm measured on the contact, against 0.8\,mm grid noise), when the contact at 0.495\,m is slower and the secondary and the lead shock are 40 and 70\,mm away, in a band of $\pm 25$\,mm. It ran to 1.3\,ms (13.8\,h on 6 processes, up to 63\,000 cells). Seeding at 60\,$\mu$s with a 1\,mm mixing layer and seeding the charge surface at $t = 0$ (as Balakrishnan and Menon \cite{balakrishnan2009}) are set up for a larger machine.

The seeded mode drops to 4.6\,mm at 250\,$\mu$s (14\%), grows exponentially with an e-folding time of about 200\,$\mu$s from 250 to 550\,$\mu$s, and from 0.6\,ms linearly by about 12.6\,mm per 100\,$\mu$s, to 113\,mm at 1.3\,ms (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g3b_seeded}). The amplitude is then 0.66 of the wavelength (172\,mm at the contact radius of 0.66\,m): the mode is nonlinear, with spikes of products into the air and bubbles of air into the products, and its first harmonic ($n = 48$) grows from about 1\,ms to 2.8 times its unseeded level. The reflected secondary shock (at the centre at about 1\,ms, outwards at about 0.9\,km/s) is at 0.27\,m at 1.3\,ms and has not reached the contact, so there is no Richtmyer--Meshkov phase yet. The mixing width grows with the perturbation, but less: it is 12\% larger than without seed at 0.6\,ms and 22\% at 1.3\,ms (118 against 97\,mm; 10 against 8\,mm per 100\,$\mu$s from 0.6\,ms). The CO burnt since 200\,$\mu$s is the same within 0.1\,g (2.80 against 2.82\,g, of 103\,g). In both runs the CO is almost constant from about 0.9\,ms: the burning stops as the mixed gas cools, not for lack of mixing surface. A strongly perturbed contact changes the shape of the fireball, but not the afterburning to 1.3\,ms. (The seeded run starts from the 1D state at 200\,$\mu$s, which holds 1.3\,g more CO than G2b at that time; G2b burnt it before 200\,$\mu$s in its wider early layer.)

\subsubsection{Turbulence and combustion closures (G3c)}
\label{sec:res_g3c}

The closures of Section~\ref{sec:mixing} were first compared on the 1D 2\,m case of Section~\ref{sec:res_e2} (full E2 physics; tutorial \path{ChargeCompBAl_1D_spherical_2m}) with a laminar gas, with $k$--$\omega$ SST on the gas, and with SST and PaSR ($C_\text{mix} = 1$), each from $t = 0$; laminar and SST to 1.3\,ms, PaSR to 0.5\,ms (about 1\,h each; PaSR 9\% slower). In 1D there is no resolved instability, so the study isolates what the closures do to a mixing layer that is spread by numerical diffusion over 1--3.5\,mm cells.

SST gives the same results as the laminar gas to 1.3\,ms: the mixing width within 0.3\,mm, the CO within 0.05\,g, and the probe pressures within plotting accuracy (Figure~\ref{fig:stage5_g3c_closures}). Its eddy viscosity is not physical: it is produced at the secondary shock (compression with a decaying $\omega$, for which OpenFOAM-12 offers no ambient limiter), reaches 0.4--1.3\,m$^2$/s, travels inwards with the shock, reflects at the centre at about 1\,ms and is at 0.24\,m at 1.3\,ms. At the contact it stays at $2$--$3\times10^{-4}$\,m$^2$/s, about ten times the molecular viscosity (1.4--3.9$\times10^{-5}$\,m$^2$/s), but the diffusion length $\sqrt{\nu_t t} \approx 0.5$\,mm in 1\,ms is below the cell size, so the numerical diffusion dominates. Without shear the model has nothing to work on; in 2D the Rayleigh--Taylor shear could produce real turbulence at the contact.

PaSR changes the afterburning, through $\kappa$ and not through the turbulence. With the RANS mixing time of 0.44--0.7\,s in the mixing layer, against chemical times of $5\times10^{-7}$\,s (60\,$\mu$s) to $3\times10^{-4}$\,s (400\,$\mu$s), the median $\kappa$ in the layer is $10^{-6}$--$7\times10^{-4}$ (maximum 0.05 to 0.4\,ms), while the products core with no turbulence keeps $\kappa = 1$. The CO peak falls from 205.4 to 199.8\,g and the 7.0\,g of CO that the laminar case burns between 0.2 and 0.5\,ms are not burnt; H$_2$ at 0.5\,ms is 3.29 instead of 3.50\,g. The peak overpressure at 0.3 and 0.5\,m is 1.9 and 1.2\% lower and the positive impulse (to 0.5\,ms) 3.3 and 5.5\%.

A mixing time from the cell size was expected to act only where the mixing layer is under-resolved. Estimated from the PaSR fields, it is $\Delta^2/(\nu + \nu_t)$ = 17--120\,ms with the SST viscosity and 2--12\,ms with a Smagorinsky viscosity of the full strain ($C_s = 0.17$), so all practical cells are under-resolved: a layer with $\tau_\text{mix} \lesssim \tau_c$ would need cells of the order of 0.05\,mm. The variant was implemented (Section~\ref{sec:mixing}) and run with a laminar gas to 0.5\,ms. Its median $\kappa$ in the layer is about ten times that of RANS PaSR to 0.2\,ms ($4\times10^{-5}$--$3\times10^{-4}$) and rises towards 1 after 0.35\,ms (0.53 at 0.5\,ms), where the chemistry has become slow and $\kappa$ no longer matters. The afterburning is suppressed as with RANS PaSR: CO peak 200.2\,g, no CO burnt from the peak to 0.5\,ms, H$_2$ 3.40\,g, peak overpressure 2.1 and 1.4\% and impulse 5.0 and 4.7\% below laminar at 0.3 and 0.5\,m. In 1D the deviatoric strain of the radial expansion is small, so this run mostly shows the molecular limit $\Delta^2/\nu$; the Smagorinsky part needs the 2D shear.

On these meshes the afterburning is therefore decided by the treatment of the mixing within a cell, not by the turbulence model. With laminar chemistry all gas that the numerical diffusion has mixed burns at the full rate; with any PaSR variant it burns at the rate of mixing within the cell, which is of the order of milliseconds. Both are limits: mixing times of a few milliseconds are of the order of the burning time of real fireballs, but three-dimensional turbulence mixes faster than the resolved 2D flow. In 1D the two limits differ by 7\,g of CO by 0.5\,ms (3.4\% of the peak), 1--2\% in peak overpressure and about 5\% in impulse at 0.3--0.5\,m. The 2D G2b case with the cell-size PaSR gives the corresponding range with the Rayleigh--Taylor shear, and experimental data (H) have to decide between the limits.

\subsection{Aluminium particles}
\label{sec:res_al}

The standalone particle model (\path{tests/0D/verify_al_particle_kinetics.py}) integrates the heating of a single particle with an internal temperature profile (Goodman heat-balance integral, parabolic profile), melting, ignition at 2050\,K, Beckstead burning and oxide condensation (Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_al_kinetics}). For a 20\,$\mu$m particle at $h = 3\times10^5\,\text{W/(m}^2\text{K)}$ in gas at 2800\,K the surface reaches the ignition temperature at 32.9\,$\mu$s, 15.5\% earlier than with a uniform particle temperature (38.9\,$\mu$s), and the Beckstead correlation gives a burn time of 1.6\,ms in air. (Until 2026-10-14 the burn-time function of this model used a dimensionally inconsistent rate constant that gave burn times of seconds; it now evaluates the published correlation.) The model has not been compared with experimental ignition delays or burn times; it serves as the reference for the solver implementation.

\paragraph{Aluminium models in the solver.} The models of Section~\ref{sec:al_impl} are verified in single-cell \texttt{blastEulerFoam} runs against independent integrations of the same model equations (Figure~\ref{fig:stage1_al_solver}). (a) 30\,$\mu$m particles at a volume fraction of $10^{-4}$, heated by gas at 2500\,K and 50\,bar through a constant heat transfer coefficient, stay at the melting point from 0.098 to 0.169\,ms, within 0.1\% of the reference, and the total energy is conserved to $4\times10^{-9}$. (b) The slip velocity of 100\,$\mu$m particles started at 600\,m/s ($Ma$ 1.7, compression branch of the Loth drag) and of 1\,$\mu$m particles at 0.2\,bar ($Re$ 5, $Kn$ 0.33, rarefaction branch) follows the reference within 0.03\% of the initial slip. (c) With the Kavanau Nusselt number a 1\,$\mu$m particle at 0.2\,bar heats with $Nu = 0.28$ instead of 2; the particle temperature follows the reference within 0.05\%, also with 300\,m/s slip and drag. (d) Ignited 1, 10 and 50\,$\mu$m particles in air at 2500\,K and 1\,atm burn out in the Beckstead time (7.3\,$\mu$s, 0.46\,ms, 8.4\,ms) within 0.23\%, and the particle mass follows $(d/d_0)^3$ within 0.04\%; particles at 1800\,K, below $T_\text{ign}$ = 2000\,K, do not burn. (e) Burning to oxide (no gas chemistry): in air the particles burn in the Beckstead time and use 0.8897\,kg O$_2$ and form 1.8899\,kg Al$_2$O$_3$ per kg Al (stoichiometry 0.8895 and 1.8895); in O$_2$/H$_2$O/CO$_2$ in N$_2$ the shares of the burning by oxidiser are 0.277/0.590/0.133 against 0.277/0.590/0.133 from $X_\text{eff}$, with H$_2$ and CO formed by the stoichiometry; a cloud with twice the stoichiometric aluminium burns until the O$_2$ is used up (burnt aluminium within 0.43\% of the stoichiometric amount); the oxide is solid in gas at 2000\,K and liquid at 2500\,K; mass is conserved to $6\times10^{-9}$ and energy, with the formation enthalpies, to $8\times10^{-7}$ of the heat released. \subsection{FOI 2013 TNT and TNT/Al charges}
\label{sec:res_foi}

\paragraph{Experiment.} In June 2013 FOI fired spherical charges of about 1\,kg of TNT and of TNT/Al 80/20 (A100 aluminium, 42\,$\mu$m), and 2\,kg of TNT, at heights of burst (HOB) of 0.15, 0.5, 1 and 2\,m, with ground-flush pressure gauges P1--P3 at 2, 4 and 6\,m from the point below the charge (1\,MHz) \cite{fedina2014,fedina2017}. Two TNT shots at the same HOB repeat to 3\% in the P2/P3 impulse and 10--11\% in the peak; at 4 and 6\,m the aluminium raises the positive impulse by 15--27\% at every HOB. The comparison here uses the pair at HOB 2\,m, shot 4 (TNT, 0.90\,kg) and shot 5 (TNT/Al, 1.05\,kg), fired 27\,min apart on the same day, so that the shot 5 -- shot 4 differences are not ambient effects. Measured at P1/P2/P3 (shot 4; shot 5): arrival 3.364/7.287/12.054\,ms (3.374/7.234/11.947), peak 440.9 (doubtful)/99.9/45.1\,kPa (326.4/123.5/37.7), positive impulse 96.9/77.8/57.3\,Pa\,s (105.5/98.8/72.8), positive duration 0.70/2.60/3.04\,ms (0.73/3.49/4.21).

\paragraph{Model.} The charges are initiated at the centre in 1D spherical geometry (products: \texttt{EuDetCode} BKW fit of the main charge with the booster lumped in; products transferred to the reactive gas below 100\,bar; gas in equilibrium above 2500\,K; for TNT/Al 90\% of the aluminium as the particle phase) and mapped at 1.5\,ms, before the shock reaches the ground, onto a 2D axisymmetric mesh with the ground as a rigid plane (Section~\ref{sec:mapping}: 5$^\circ$ wedge, $7.04 \times 5.12$\,m, 32\,mm base cells, AMR to 4\,mm at the shock and 8\,mm in the products/air mixing layer, 2\,kPa pressure jumps per face; about 8\,h on 192 cores to 18\,ms). The products/air mixing at the fireball edge, which a 4\,mm mesh does not resolve, is represented by the subgrid mixing model of Section~\ref{sec:mixing_model}.

\paragraph{TNT (shot 4).} The TNT shot sets the resolution and the mixing constant before the aluminium is added.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Resolution.} With the shock at 8, 4 and 2\,mm the laminar P2/P3 peaks are 76.9/39.6, 80.4/41.4 and 80.8/43.0\,kPa with an unchanged impulse (72.4--72.8 and 48.5--48.6\,Pa\,s): 4\,mm is converged at P2, which stays 19\% below the measurement, and resolution explains less than a quarter of the P2 peak deficit and none of the impulse deficit. 4\,mm is used for all further runs.
    \item \emph{Secondary shock.} The inward-moving shock of the products, reflected at the centre, reaches the gauges as a secondary shock, measured 2.66, 3.01 and 3.36\,ms after the main shock at P1, P2 and P3 (jumps of 30, 15 and 6\,kPa; the TNT shots at 0.5\,m show 2.5--4.3\,ms). Its timing is mesh-independent in 1D (3.80/4.73\,ms after the main shock at 2.83/4.47\,m from 32 to 2\,mm cells, laminar), its strength is not (the jump at 2.83\,m grows from 1.1 to 12\,kPa from 32 to 2\,mm), and in 2D it is not refined by the AMR on its way to P2/P3, so only its timing is used. Without mixing it is about 1.2 and 1.8\,ms late at P1 and P2. The mixing (Section~\ref{sec:mixing_model}) burns the products in the fireball and advances it (Figure~\ref{fig:foi_shot4}a): from 3.87/4.82\,ms after the main shock at 2.83/4.47\,m (laminar) to 3.08/3.84, 2.73/3.38, 2.51/3.09 and 2.19/2.65\,ms for $C$ = 0.005, 0.01, 0.015 and 0.03. The measured timing at P1 is met at $C \approx 0.0115$ and at P2 at $C \approx 0.016$; $C = 0.0125$ is used. The 1D timing carries over to the 2D ground case ($C = 0.005$: 3.1\,ms at P1 in 2D, 3.08\,ms at 2.83\,m in 1D).
    \item \emph{Main shock.} Relative to $C = 0.005$, larger $C$ advances the main shock at 4.47\,m by 37, 73, 140 and 311\,$\mu$s for $C$ = 0.0075, 0.01, 0.015 and 0.03 and raises its positive impulse by 3, 5, 10 and 39\% (Figure~\ref{fig:foi_shot4}b); at $C = 0.03$ the secondary shock arrives within the positive phase.
    \item \emph{2D results} (Table~\ref{tab:foi_shot4}, Figure~\ref{fig:foi_shot4}c, d). The mixing moves the shock arrival towards the measurement (P2: 271, 190 and 79\,$\mu$s late for laminar, $C = 0.005$ and 0.0125) and raises the impulse. With $C = 0.0125$ P3 agrees within 7\,$\mu$s in arrival, 0.5\% in peak and 5\% in impulse. At P2 the impulse is 14\% high because the secondary shock arrives before the overpressure has turned negative, so the positive phase runs on through the secondary pulse (positive duration 4.0 against 2.6\,ms); in the measurement the overpressure reaches $-4$\,kPa before the secondary shock. This also holds for shot 5 and makes the positive duration unsuitable for the comparison. The P2 peak is 17\% low with every closure.
\end{itemize}
The mixing constant is therefore set by the timing of the secondary shock and not by the burnt fraction: $C = 0.0125$ burns about 2.5 times the products fuel of the history of Balakrishnan et al.\ ($C = 0.005$; 1D, 3\,ms). It is calibrated on one shot at one height of burst.

\paragraph{The aluminium effect.} The measured shot 5 -- shot 4 differences (Table~\ref{tab:foi_al}) show the character of the aluminium effect: a small change of the shock arrival ($-53$ and $-107$\,$\mu$s at P2/P3), a peak change of either sign ($-26$/$+24$/$-16$\%), and an impulse gain of 27\% at P2 and P3 that comes mainly 2--3.5\,ms after the shock (at P2 $+3.0/+0.9/+1.7/+0.5/+1.0/+6.3/+6.0$\,Pa\,s in successive 0.5\,ms windows after arrival), together with an earlier secondary shock (at P2 2.11 against 3.01\,ms after the main shock). Several stages of the aluminium model were needed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item With ignition at 2050\,K and the cut-off $\alpha_\text{Al} = 10^{-6}$ (below which the ignited fraction and the diameter were reset), the model gave about the measured impulse gain but put it into the main shock: arrival $-310$/$-427$\,$\mu$s at P2/P3 and the gain in the first 2\,ms. An energy source of 0.4\,$e_0 m$ added to the products gas of the TNT case at 0.5--1.5\,ms reproduced the measured arrival shift and the late gain, which pointed to aluminium energy released in the fireball, not ahead of it.
    \item A particle-number check then showed that the reacting diameter model removed particles numerically (in 1D 58\% of the particles were gone by 16\,ms although the burn time of 42\,$\mu$m particles is 5--7\,ms in air): the ignited fraction and the diameter were advanced from the unblended stage value with RK2SSP, which transported them 1.5 times faster than the particles, and the mass transfer included the fall of the cell-mean diameter by transport. After the fix (UPDATES 4.5) the aluminium burnt only 4.5\% (2050\,K) and the 2D effect was that of inert mass ($+1$\% impulse): the fireball gas reaches at most 2000--2150\,K, and the ignited fraction was lost where the cloud diluted below $10^{-6}$ (42\% of the aluminium mass at 3\,ms).
    \item Lowering the burning cut-off to $10^{-9}$ (the phase \texttt{residualAlpha} and the drag cut-offs stay at $10^{-6}$; $10^{-9}$ there is unstable) lets the ignited particles burn in the dilute cloud; with $T_\text{ign} = 933$\,K the burning continues to 95\% and produces the late pulse and the earlier secondary shock, but the burning of the first millisecond still drives the main shock (1D: $-176$\,$\mu$s, $+20$\% peak).
    \item The ignition delay of Section~\ref{sec:al_ignition} removes that early burning. In 1D, with $T_\text{ign} = 933$\,K, $\tau_\text{ign} = 0.5$\,ms brings the arrival shift at 4.47\,m to $-78$\,$\mu$s; $T_\text{ign} = 1500$\,K with any delay gives no ignition.
\end{itemize}

In 2D with $C = 0.0125$, $T_\text{ign} = 933$\,K, the burning cut-off $10^{-9}$ and $\tau_\text{ign} = 0.25$, 0.5 and 1\,ms, the aluminium burns 20, 15 and 6\% by 1.5\,ms, 41, 36 and 25\% by 3\,ms and 94, 94 and 90\% by 18\,ms. Table~\ref{tab:foi_al} and Figure~\ref{fig:foi_waveforms} compare the result with the measurement. With $\tau_\text{ign} = 0.5$\,ms the arrival shifts agree within 3\,$\mu$s at the three gauges, the impulse gain is 23--25\% (measured 9\% at P1 and 27\% at P2/P3) and comes mainly 2.5--3.5\,ms after arrival (at P2 $+6.1$\,Pa\,s at 2.5--3\,ms, measured $+6.3$), and the secondary shock comes 0.46\,ms earlier than for shot 4 at P2 (measured 0.90\,ms). The arrival fixes the delay: 0.25 and 1\,ms move it by about $-40$ and $+75$--$95$\,$\mu$s at P2/P3, while the late gain hardly changes ($+6.8$ and $+5.1$\,Pa\,s at 2.5--3\,ms). The peak gain (+7 to +9\% at all gauges with 0.5\,ms) does not follow the measured peaks, which differ in sign from gauge to gauge; the absolute P2 peak is low for both shots (89.9 and 82.6\,kPa against 123.5 and 99.9). The delay is fitted to this pair of shots; it lies within the measured delays for 36--54\,$\mu$m particles in detonation products (Section~\ref{sec:al_ignition}), its temperature- and size-dependent form does not reproduce the measurement (below). The other TNT/Al shots (HOB 0.15--1\,m) are the test of the calibrated model.

\paragraph{Kuhl--Boiko delay.} The temperature- and size-dependent delay of Eq.~(\ref{eq:kuhlboiko}) was run for shot 5 in 1D (7\,m, $C = 0.005$, otherwise as the constant-delay run): $\tau = 2.5\times10^{-11}\,\text{s}\,\exp(30\,000\,\text{K}/T_\text{gas})\,(d/4\,\mu\text{m})^{n}$ with $n = 0$, 2 and 2.3, and with $n = 2$ in the mixture temperature, all with $T_\text{ign} = 0$. Table~\ref{tab:foi_kb} compares them with the constant delays. With the size scaling the delay of the 42\,$\mu$m particles is 110 ($n = 2$) or 220 ($n = 2.3$) times that of the 4\,$\mu$m reference, about 0.7\,ms at 2400\,K and 9\,ms at 2000\,K, while the gas around the aluminium is much cooler: weighted by the aluminium mass, $T_\text{gas}$ is 1030, 760 and 580\,K at 0.5, 1 and 1.5\,ms, 8\% of the aluminium is in gas above 2000\,K at 0.5\,ms and none after 1.5\,ms. The mean ignition progress stops at 0.16 ($n = 2$) and 0.08 ($n = 2.3$), less than 2\% of the aluminium burns by 16\,ms, and the result is that of inert aluminium. The mixture temperature makes no difference. Without the size scaling the delay is short while the products are hot (mean progress 0.5 at 0.5\,ms), the aluminium ignites early and the result is that of a constant delay of 0.25\,ms: the arrival shifts are 80--100\,$\mu$s larger than measured. In 1D an Arrhenius delay in the gas temperature is thus significant only in the first 0.5\,ms; it gives either no aluminium effect or an early one, not the ignition at 0.5--1.5\,ms that the measurement needs. Ignition at that time would need the particles to be heated again, for instance when they are carried back into the fireball in the negative phase \cite{posey2021}, which needs the 2D/3D mixing. The 2D check, shot 5 as the constant-delay run of Table~\ref{tab:foi_al} but with the $n = 2$ delay, shows no such reheating: 0.3\% of the aluminium has burnt at the 1.5\,ms mapping, and up to 3.75\,ms (where the run was stopped) no aluminium ignites; the ignition progress stays below 0.3. The hottest gas in the domain is about 1950\,K, where the delay of a 42\,$\mu$m particle is about 13\,ms, and weighted by the aluminium mass the gas temperature is 490--620\,K at 1.5--3.75\,ms, with 5--7\% of the aluminium in gas above 1500\,K and none above 2000\,K. The Kuhl--Boiko delay with its size scaling thus gives the effect of inert aluminium in 2D as well, and the fitted constant delay remains the working model.

\subsection{Open issues}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Aluminium ignition.} The FOI shot 5 result needs a low ignition temperature (933\,K) with a delay of about 0.5\,ms; the delay is fitted to one pair of shots and moves the arrival by 40--95\,$\mu$s per factor of two (Section~\ref{sec:res_foi}). It lies within the delays measured in H$_2$/O$_2$ detonation products for particles of this size, while nitromethane/Al cylinders react within 50\,$\mu$s (Section~\ref{sec:al_ignition}). The temperature- and size-dependent (Kuhl--Boiko) delay does not reproduce it: with its size scaling the 42\,$\mu$m particles do not ignite in 1D or 2D (the gas around them is below 2000\,K after 0.5\,ms), without it they ignite too early (Table~\ref{tab:foi_kb}). A high ignition temperature (2050\,K) is never reached in the 2D fireball and gives the effect of inert mass.
    \item \emph{Burning cut-off.} Burning in the dilute cloud needs a cut-off of $10^{-9}$; the phase \texttt{residualAlpha} and the drag cut-offs cannot be lowered below $10^{-6}$ without instability, so particles in cells below $10^{-6}$ burn while their drag and phase numerics treat them as residual.
    \item \emph{Peak overpressure.} At 4\,m the TNT peak is 19\% below the measurement at a converged resolution (4\,mm), and the TNT/Al peak 27\%; the measured shot 5 -- shot 4 peak changes differ in sign between the gauges ($-26$/$+24$/$-16$\%) and are not reproduced ($+7$ to $+9$\%). The impulse agrees within 5\% at 6\,m, but is 14\% high at 4\,m because the secondary shock, which is timed by the mixing constant, arrives while the overpressure is still positive.
    \item \emph{Subgrid mixing.} $C = 0.0125$ fits the secondary-shock timing of the TNT shot and burns about 2.5 times the products fuel of the Balakrishnan et al.\ history ($C = 0.005$). Whether the constant carries over to other heights of burst, charge masses and resolved 2D/3D mixing is open; with a resolved mixing layer $D$ would have to scale with the cell size.
    \item \emph{Earlier aluminium results.} The Comp-B/Al results on 2\,m (Section~\ref{sec:res_e2}: 36--52\% higher peaks beyond 1\,m from the aluminium) were obtained before the stage-update correction of the reacting diameter model and with \texttt{alphaMin} $\ge 10^{-6}$; they have to be repeated.
    \item The gas carbon of the 2D FOI mixing run grows by $7.6\times10^{-4}$ from 1.5 to 18\,ms (laminar $1.1\times10^{-4}$), possibly from the AMR mapping of the early mixing layer.
    \item Total energy in the spherical cases is conserved to $4$--$7\times10^{-4}$, not yet to round-off (most of the error arises at the end of the programmed burn).
    \item The first mixed cell at the charge/air interface reaches the 50\,000\,K decode cap during breakout (gas at ambient density next to products at 0.7\,GPa).
    \item Blast against Kingery--Bulmash (Section~\ref{sec:res_e1}): with 1\,mm cells the TNT peak overpressure is within 3.4\% for $Z \ge 4$ but 5--8\% low at $Z$ = 1.5--3, and the positive impulse is 14--17\% low without afterburning.
    \item Programmed burn needs a products EOS whose CJ speed is not above the burn speed; the Comp-B/Al cases run slightly faster than the CJ speed of their products EOS and sit on the weak branch (2.4\% below $p_\text{CJ}$). A checker (\path{utilities/thermo_fitter/check_programmed_burn.py}) compares the burn speed with the products EOS for any case.
    \item 2D Comp-B/Al cases: Rayleigh--Taylor fingers grow from the mesh (not converged), and the inward secondary shock runs ahead along the axis and the symmetry plane (grid-seeded; Section~\ref{sec:res_g2}). The cylinders (L/D 1 and 4) and the seeded-contact variants are parked for a larger machine.
    \item Closures (Section~\ref{sec:res_g3c}): $k$--$\omega$ SST has no effect in 1D and produces a spurious eddy viscosity at the secondary shock; PaSR suppresses the afterburning in numerically mixed cells. The FOI cases use the laminar closure with the subgrid mixing model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Plan}

The plan is ordered by the path to a validated model (details and run data in \path{report/development_plan.md}, Section~6):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Aluminium ignition}: a physical basis for the fitted delay. The Kuhl--Boiko delay in the gas temperature fails in 1D and 2D because the gas around the particles cools below 2000\,K within 0.5\,ms, where the delay of 42\,$\mu$m particles exceeds 10\,ms. Candidates: a delay in the particle temperature (the particles stay near 900--1000\,K for about 2\,ms), a delay that depends on the oxidiser (products/air mixing), or the low-temperature cloud ignition of Boiko and Poplavski.
    \item \emph{FOI TNT/Al at the other heights of burst} (shots 6, 7 and 8 at 1, 0.5 and 0.15\,m, with the TNT shots 3, 2/9 and 1): the independent test of the calibrated mixing constant and ignition model, which mapping earlier onto finer 2D meshes makes more expensive.
    \item \emph{Repeat the earlier aluminium cases} (Comp-B/Al spherical and 2\,m) with the corrected reacting diameter model.
    \item \emph{Fireball temperature}: the FOI pyrometer (aimed about 1.5\,m up at 2\,m radius; TNT/Al up to about 1650\,K) against the model.
    \item \emph{Peak overpressure}: the 19\% deficit at 4\,m (resolution is excluded; the ground reflection, the products EOS and the booster are candidates).
    \item \emph{Pontalier et al.} gelled nitromethane/Al free-field trials (1D tutorial in place): a second dataset, free field and without the ground.
    \item \emph{Particle method and multi-dimensional mixing}: the Eulerian/Lagrangian comparison (Stage 4), resolved 2D/3D mixing with a cell-size-dependent closure, and the parked cylinder and seeded runs; radiation where significant.
\end{enumerate}
Items 1--3 use the existing FOI chain on the cluster (one 2D shot in about 8\,h on 192 cores).
